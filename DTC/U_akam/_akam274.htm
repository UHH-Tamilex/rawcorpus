TRANSLATION
  • (Charioteer)! The sweet village of our gentle-natured young talivi (14)
  • who adorns herself with fragrant arabian jasmine flowers as a mark of her chastity (13)
  • is situated in the cool and fragrant forest region. At midnight when the winter season, has arrived and stayed (3),
  • the clouds (2)
  • have thundered for the big sky to reverberate (1),
  • the drops combined with the rapid descent of increasing rin (2)
  • cused the snakes pin (1).
  • (At that time) the shepherd who is standing lonely and who has fixed his staff between his legs firmly (8)
  • is drenched on one side by the tiny drops of rin water (7),
  • holds in his hand a hoop supported by strong ropes, an earthern pot and a bed made of leather (6)
  • lights (6)
  • the small spark of fire got by friction of two sticks, in the firewood (5).
  • He intends to afford protection to the flock of sheep of moving heads (4),
  • whistles twisting his lips (9).
  • The sound travels fast (9)
  • and reaches the ears of the small fox which is on the look-out for carrying a hopping lamp (10).
  • It is frigtened and fleets to hide itself in the short thorny shrubbery (11),

NOTES

It is a literary convention that cobras are afrid of the harsh noise of thunder.

Women noted for their chastity adorn themselves with the white flowers of arabian jasmine as it signifies purity. mullaicāṉṟakaṟpu may be interpreted in this way also; chastity which makes talivi to stay in her hose putting up with separation from talivaṉ; then naṟumalar is an epithet given to mullai without the idea of the creeper. Women grow the creeper, arabian jasmine as an emblem of chastity; "tēvimullai vaḷarttaṟkuk kāraṇam kaṟpuṭaiṭaiyeṉa uṇark (takkayākap paraṇi, 75, urai); "kaṟpiṉ mikuti tōṉṟa mullai cūṭutalaṭ marapu (ciṟupāṇāṟṟuppaṭai, 28-30, nacciṉārkkiṭaiyar urai) ""mullai cāṉṟa kaṟpiṉ melliyal'' (ciṟupāṇāṟṟuppaṭai, 30);

The last two lines are to be found without any change in naṟṟiṇi, 142, ll.10-11; The author of that poem is also iṭikkātaṉār 
