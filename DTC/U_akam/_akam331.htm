TRANSLATION
  • Having gone beyond the dried up curam as cruel as the sharp edge of the sword (14)
  • which titiyaṉ drew from its scabbard in order to fight with the vēḷir (petty chieftains), (13),
  • who rejoiced after consuming liquor and was seated in the assembly hall in the early morning (12)
  • after giving away always (9)
  • munificently many ornaments to pāṇar (11)
  • who could play upon the little yāḻ with the black body who had mastered the difficult intricacies of music (10),
  • to make them shout vocifarously (out of joy) (11),
  • to leave behind many regions with small villages (8)
  • where the girls of toddy sellers wearing garments made of tender leaves (5),
  • collect in the long hollows of bamboos of round joints (6)
  • the leavings of the abudant white flowers of the iruppai (iruppai mahua tree) tree of red leaves and trunks of tall stature, which resemble the tusk turned in a lathe and shaped, like molucca beans (kaḻaṅku Kaḻaṅku) which resembles the tusk turned in stripping them from the branches, by the group of female bears which gave birth to young ones, which spread themselves like goats, wander to sell them going to every street in the small hamlets situated in the hills (7),
  • our talaivar who parted from us (has no love towards us (9)
  • (what shall we do ?)

NOTES

vāṉpū white flowers;

kōṭu here means the tusk of elephants; tusks of elephants that are turned in a lathe to make artificial Kaḻanku; this is mentioned in this anthology itself in two places; ""āṭutaḷir iruppaik kūṭu kuvi vāṉpūk, kōṭukaṭai kaḻaṅkiṉ aṟai micait tāam'', ""kuvimukiḻ iruppai, maruppuk kaṭaintaṉṉa koḷḷai vāṉpū'' (akam, 135-8-9, 267-6-7).

That the bears are partial towards the flowers of iruppai is found in this anthology 81-8, 95-6-9, 149-3-4, 171-13-15, 247-4-5, 267-6-8, 275-11-12.

There is a proverb in tamaiḻ which means the flowers of iruppai are jaggery for villages ("ālai illā ūrukku iruppaippūc carkkarai) which have no sugar-cane; so the little girls of toddy-sellers collected the flowers left by bears and sold them in the village of hilly tract.

In line 9 the finite verb can be changed into a adverbial participe and rendered as aṉpilarāyc ceṉṟōr.

From this poem we learn that titiyaṉ was a patron of musicians (pāṇar) and rewarded for their proficiency in music.

There is a variant reading tōṭu ciṉai uraii; tōṭu is a better reading than cēṭu.

There is a meaning given to paḻaiyar makaḷir in the old gloss; it denotes women of hunters and women of ploughmen.

vaṟuñcuram (l.14) is a better reading as it is keeping with moṉai (mōṉai) in "vāy vāy'
