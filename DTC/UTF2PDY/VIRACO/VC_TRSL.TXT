             Prolegomenon (Pāyiram) 

 

The Special Preface relating to the Invocation and the Theme 

 

            Bearing on his crown the blessed feet of Lord Buddha the lofty one, the one enthroned under the Bodhi tree, the one endowed with multi-foliate powers acquired through asceticism, the one who by reason of his great excellence resulting from asceticism is not to be matched by any one, the one who is to be counted pure in mind and word and body he has essayed to incite the grammar on Tamil. He is indeed Buddha Mitthiram, the prince of the realm of Ponpaṟṟi grit with green gardens. [The author here speaks of himself in the third person.] 

 

            The Apology (Avi-y-aṭakkam) 

 

            If I am questioned: "Can you speak on that Tamil grammar, the work wrought for the benefit of them that live in the world where abides his immortal fame, the work which is so cooling to the mind, the work of Akattiyaṉ who is endowed with clarified intellect, the work of the chēla (pupil) of Avalōkaṉ whose cultural attinment is hiled by many discerning scholars", I will answer thus: "The fly too wings the spacious sky expanse where flies the Brahminy kite!  In this is the fitness of things? What does the world say?  (Does the world approve of this?) If this can pass muster, so can my work also."

 

            The Name of the Work 

 

            Tamil grammar deals with the vast pentad of letters, word, subject-matter ({i}poruḷ{/i}), prosody and figures (orthography, etymology, poetics, poetry and rhetoric) which abide in the tongues of poets and which are the essential parts of poems. Briefly have I treated these indicating here and there the ancient rules of Sanskrit grammar too. As Vīracōḻaṉ whose chariot in decked with flower garlands has cused this to be written, I christen this Vīracōḻiyam.

First Part: The Section on Letters: The Chapter on the combination of letters. 

 

On the triad of vowels, consonants and āytham.

 

1.         If the known and articulated letters, the twelve beginning with  {i}a{/i} and ending with {i}au{/i} are called vowels and the eighteen beginning with {i}k{/i} and ending with {i}ṉ{/i} are called consonants. That letter which is sui generis and poised midway between the vowels and the consonants is called the āytham. In the 18 consonants the six letters {i}y r l v ḻ ḷ{/i} are medial consonants, the six letters: {i}ṅ ñ ṇ n m{/i} and {i}ṉ{/i} are soft consonants and the remining six letters: {i}k c ṭ t p{/i} and {i}ṟ{/i} are harsh consonants. 

 

The short and long vowel-consonants, the vowel elongation, relative letters and the over-short {i}u{/i}. 

 

 2.             Vowel-consonants which have a consonant as the end-letter are arranged in eighteen strands. In each strand of the vowel-consonants devoid of the consonant, five are designated "short letters" and seven as "long letters". The vowel elongation is a still further elongation of the long letter. Hard and soft consonants in combination come out in pirs and are known as "relative letters". At the end of a long letter or a word made up of several letters, if the letter {i}u{/i} unites with the six hard consonants {i}k c ṭ t p{/i} and {i}ṟ{/i} that {i}u{/i} gets shortened and is known as "over-short {i}u{/i}". 

 

{i}au{/i} and {i}ai{/i}; interrogative letters, letters which get dotted, demonstrative letters and the over-short {i}u{/i}. 

 

3.             {i}au{/i} results when {i}a{/i} unites with {i}u{/i}; when {i}a{/i} unites with {i}i, ai{/i} is produced. The letters {i}ā ē{/i} and {i}ō{/i} imply interrogation only when they form the end-letters. {i}ē{/i} and {i}ō{/i} as well as the 18 consonants are known as dotted letters.[1] The letters {i}a i{/i} and {i}u,{/i} when employed demonstratively at the beginning of a word, are called demonstrative letters. When the over-short {i}u{/i} combines with {i}y{/i} as the initial letters of the juxtapused word, the over-short {i}u{/i} becomes {i}i{/i}. That {i}i{/i} gets dwindled in its {i}māttiri{/i} and is called the over-short {i}i{/i}.

 

Interrogative letters, coalescing of two mute consonants and coalescing of three mute consonants

 

 4.        The letters {i}e ē{/i} and {i}yā{/i}- which imply interrogation come as the first letters. The letters getting born by reason of fourfold accent – loud, soft middling and whooping and undergoing coalescing of two mute consonants and occasionally three mute consonants as the case may be, when conjoined with themselves or other consonants. Thus say the wise. 

 

The {i}māttiris{/i} of the letters and the conjunction of the first vowel of the succeeding word with the last consonant of the preceding word

 

5.         The time-measures of the letters are as follows: 

            (1) Short vowel ... 1 

            (2) Long vowel ... 2 

            (3) Among these, for {i}ai{/i} and {i}au{/i} ... 1½ 

            (4) Consonant, {i}āytham{/i}, over-short {i}i{/i} and over-short {i}u{/i} ... ½ 

            (5) Prolonged vowels ... 3 

            (6) The measure of the vowel-consonant is the measure of its vowel (only). 

            The vowel which forms the first letter of the succeeding word will combine with the consonant, that is, the last letter of the preceding word, becoming in the process a vowel-consonant. 

 

The places of the genesis of letters and the effort needed therefore

 

 6.        The wind rising from the navel courses through chest, head, throat and nose. When it gets expressed, it assumes different sounds by reasons of the activation of palate, teeth, tongue and lips. Hard consonants, {i}āytham{/i}, medial consonants and vowels as well as soft consonants get born respectively in chest, head, throat and nose. 

 

The Initial letters

 

7.         The twelve vowels, all the vowel-consonants of {i}ka ca ta na pa{/i} and {i}ma{/i}, eight vowel-consonants of {i}va{/i}, six vowel-consonants of {i}ya{/i} are the initial letters of the Tamil words. 

 

Finals

 

 8.        Of soft consonants the letters {i}ṉ{/i}, {i}m{/i} and {i}ṇ{/i}, of medial consonants the five letters other than {i}v{/i} and of vowels the ten letters other than {i}e{/i} and {i}o{/i} are the finals of Tamil words. 

 

Natural combination and mutational combination

 

9.         When the first letter of the succeeding word combines with the last letter of the receding word, any one of the following may result:

            (1) Natural combination. 

            (2) Insertion. 

            (3) Transmutation and 

            (4) Omission. 

            All the three results (2 - 4) are sometimes noticeable in single words also. 

 

Mutational combinations according to Sanskrit

 

 10.             Mutational combinations may occur (in Sanskrit) in a single word or letter. There are therefore six combinations. Agin, as the occurrence may be in the beginning or he middle or the end, eighteen. These changes are according to usage only.  

 

The changes occurring to the letter {i}na{/i} which conveys the negative sense and the changes to the letters {i}ē{/i} and {i}ō{/i} in Sanskrit

 

11.       In Sanskrit, if before the letter the negative {i}na{/i} a word beginning with a vowel comes, {i}na{/i} becomes {i}an{/i}; it becomes {i}a{/i} in the case of a consonant. {i}ē{/i} and {i}ō{/i} of Sanskrit become {i}ai{/i} and {i}au{/i} in Tamil. 

 

The combinations in Sanskrit

 

  12.             {i}Virutthi{/i} and {i}guṇam{/i} are the combinations in Sanskrit. In noun-roots and verb-roots {i}a{/i} becomes {i}ā{/i}, {i}i{/i} becomes {i}ai,{/i} {i}u{/i} becomes {i}au{/i} and {i}ṛ{/i} becomes {i}ār{/i}. These changes are known as {i}virutthi{/i}. Similarly, when {i}i{/i} becomes {i}e{/i} and {i}u{/i} becomes {i}o{/i} the changes are known as {i}guṇam{/i}. 

 

The combinations in Tamil – vowel before vowel and instances of omission – relating to last letters of preceding words

 

13.       If preceding words ending in {i}i ī{/i} and {i}ai{/i} are combined with succeeding words beginning with a vowel, the consonantal glide comes into being. In the case of other words {i}v{/i} is the gliding consonant. In the case of {i}ē{/i} alone, either {i}y{/i} or {i}v{/i} will be the gliding consonant. When succeeding words combine with preceding words, omission of a syllable or a vowel-consonant or a vowel or a consonant may occur. 

 

The joining of {i}t{/i} and {i}n{/i} which are the first letters of a succeeding word with {i}ṇ{/i}, the last letter of the preceding word; the joining of the vowel which is the first letter of the succeeding word with the last letter, a consonant following a short vowel or vowel-consonant; and the joining of hard consonants of the succeeding word with {i}y r ḻ{/i} of the preceding word

 

14.       In the above cases when {i}t{/i} combines with {i}ṇ{/i} the former changes into {i}ṇ{/i}. When to first letter of the succeeding word which is a vowel combines with the last letter (consonant) of the preceding word made up of two letters, namely, a short vowel or vowel-consonant and a consonant (only), then the consonant at the end gets doubled. When the hard consonant (the first letter) of the succeeding word joins with is {i}y r{/i} {i}ḻ{/i} – the end letters – of the preceding word, either the hard consonant or its corresponding soft consonant gets inserted between the two words. 

 

{i}n{/i} joining with {i}i ī ai{/i} of the preceding word; {i}t{/i} joining words ending in {i}ḻ{/i} and {i}ṉ{/i}

Hard consonants and {i}n{/i} (the first letter) of the succeeding word joining the end-letter {i}ṉ{/i} of the preceding word; {i}ṉ{/i} preceded by a single long vowel or long vowel-consonant joining {i}t{/i} (the first letter) of the succeeding word

Hard consonants, {i}n{/i} and {i}y{/i} joining {i}l{/i} the end-letter of the preceding word; {i}n{/i} joining the end-letter, {i}y{/i}

When a hard consonant which is the first letter of the succeeding word joins the end-letter {i}l{/i} of the preceding word, {i}l{/i} becomes {i}ṟ.{/i} When the hard consonant is {i}t{/i}, it also becomes {i}ṟ.{/i} When {i}n{/i} joins the end-letter {i}l{/i} of the preceding word, both become {i}ṉ{/i}. The Section on Words 

 

The chapter relating to cases 

 

The group of cases and the group of {i}kārakams{/i}

 

29.             Beginning with the nominative there are eight-fold cases of noun. [{i}kārakam{/i} covers nouns with case-endings having a syntactical link with verbs.] 

             {i}Kārakams{/i} are six in number. They are (1) the nominative (2) the objective (3) the instrumental (4) the dative (5) the ablative and (6) the locative. 

 

The classification of nouns and the case-endings of the nominative case

 

30.       Nouns are eightfold. They relate to (1) male singular (2) female singular (3) plural (4) neuter singular (5) sentient plural (6) honorific singular (7) honorific plural and (8) honorific neuter singular. 

            The case-endings of the nominative case are the seven particles which are {i}r{/i}, {i}ar{/i}, {i}ār{/i}, {i}arkaḷ{/i}, {i}ārkaḷ{/i}, {i}kaḷ{/i} and {i}mār{/i}. These particles can be retined even when the other case-endings are combined with them. (2) 

 

The 64 {i}kāraka{/i} words

 

31.       The aforesid eight roots ({i}pirakiruti{/i}) with their combination of the case-endings which are eightfold yield 64 {i}kāraka{/i} words in Tamil. (3) 

 

The {i}kāraka{/i} words in Sankskrit

 

            32. In Sanskrit there are three genders: the male, the female and the neuter. There are seven case-endings from first to seventh. In numbers there are three categories: singular, dual and plural. Nouns are twofold, the vowel-ending and the consonant-ending. If these are multiplied (3x7x3x2) we get 126 {i}kāraka{/i} words. 

 

The nominative case-ending joiningthe eightfold root

 

33.      {i}cu, ar, ār, arkaḷ, ārkaḷ, kaḷ{/i} and {i}mār{/i}; these seven are the nominative case-endings. The masculine singular the feminine singular and the neuter singular will have {i}cu{/i} as the case-ending. In neuter plural {i}cu{/i} and {i}kaḷ{/i} will be the two case-endings. The case-endings of the honorific masculine and feminine singulars will cover as instances save {i}kaḷ{/i}. In all places the particle {i}cu{/i} will disappear when the noun joins a word. (5) 


 Case-endings of cases 2 to 4 and what they mean

 

 34.      {i}ai{/i} in the case-ending of the second case and in the objective case. The omission of this case-ending will not alter the meaning, generally. The case-endings of the third case are {i}oṭu{/i}, {i}oṭu{/i} and {i}āl{/i}. These stand for the subject or the instrument. The case-endings of the fourth case are {i}ku{/i} and {i}poruṭṭu{/i}. The fourth case is also the dative case. (6) 

 

The cases 5, 6 and 7

 

35.       The case-ending of the fifth case is {i}niṉṟu{/i} which combines with the case-ending of the seventh case, namely, {i}il{/i}. It means limit. The sixth case is the possessive/ genitive case. Its case-endings are: {i}uṭiyiṉ{/i}, {i}uṭiyār{/i}, {i}uṭiyārkaḷ{/i}, {i}uṭiyatu{/i} and {i}uṭiyiṉa{/i}. {i}uṭiya{/i} is also included sometimes. The sixth case is not a {i}kārakam{/i}. If it is to be governed by a verb, {i}ku{/i} will be the case-ending. The seventh is the locative case. Its meaning is base or support. Its case-endings are {i}kē{/i}, {i}uḻi{/i}, {i}vayiṉ{/i}, etc. (7) 

 

The case-endings of the vocative case

 

36.       While the other cases are common for the First, Second and Third Persons, vocative case refers only to the Second Person. Its case-endings are {i}āy{/i}, {i}āḷ{/i}, {i}ī{/i}, {i}ē{/i}, {i}a{/i}, {i}ā{/i}, {i}āṉ{/i}, {i}ōl{/i}, {i}ōy{/i} and {i}īr{/i}. In some words the last consonant gets omitted (in this case). (8)

The chapter relating to {i}kārakam{/i} 

 

38.       Nouns with case-endings governed by verbs are {i}kārakam{/i}. These are six in number. The doer of an action is the subject. The action or the result of the action is the object. That which is used for the performance of action is the instrument. The removal of a thing from something is {i}avati{/i}. The place of an action is location. The receiver of a thing is {i}kōḷi{/i}. The chapter on ellipsis definition of ellipsis; the case ellipsis and its kinds

 

44.             Ellipsis is the combination of two or more nouns resulting in a compound word. In case ellipsis the case-endings get omitted without {i}jeopardy{/i} to the meaning of the compound word. In case ellipsis many are the instances where only the case-endings get omitted; in a few other instances the omission relates to the case-endings as well as the governing verbs. There are also certin very rare instances where besides the case-endings and their corresponding governing verbs, some other nouns too get omitted. (1) 

 

  The six kinds of ellipsis in Sanskrit

 

  45.     (1) {i}tatpuruṣaṉ{/i} (case ellipsis.)  It is of eight kinds, namely, case-endings 1 to 7 and {i}naññu{/i} (the case-ending which conveys the opposite sense). 

            (2) {i}pahuvirīki{/i} (metonymical ellipsis) 

            (3) {i}karmatārayaṉ{/i} (adjectival ellipsis) 

            (4) {i}tuvantuvaṉ{/i} (cumulative ellipsis) 

            (5) {i}tviku{/i} (number ellipsis) 

            (6) {i}avyayipāvam{/i} (particle ellipsis) (2) 

 

Case ellipsis and number ellipsis

 

46.       Case ellipsis is of eight kinds. Vide 45. In number ellipsis, the number comes as the preceding word followed by a noun. It is of two kinds: singular and plural. (3) 

 

Metonymical ellipsis and its kinds

 

47.       This is of seven kinds: 

             (1)Ellipsis in two words, (2) Ellipsis in many words, (3) Ellipsis having a number in the succeeding word, (4) Ellipsis having a number in both the preceding and succeeding words, (5) Ellipsis where a word is repeated, (6) Ellipsis where the word {i}caka{/i} comes as the preceding word, (7) Ellipsis relating to directions. 

            In metonymical ellipsis the meaning of the resulting compound word abides beyond that word (4) 

 

Adjectival ellipsis and its kinds

 

48.       This is of six kinds. 

            (1) The preceding word is an adjective of quality. (2) The succeeding word refers to quality. (3) The preceding word is a comparison. (4) The succeeding word is a comparison. (5) The preceding word refers to intention. (6) The preceding word refers to certinty. (5) 

 

The particle ellipsis and the cumulative ellipsis

 

49.       The particle ellipsis is of two kinds: (1) The particle being the preceding word, (2) The particle being the succeeding word. 

            The cumulative ellipsis is of two kinds: (1) Referring to the personal class, (2) referring to the impersonal class. (6) 

 

The six ellipses in Tamil and the kinds of adjectival and cumulative ellipses

 

50.       The elliptical compounds are of six kinds, namely, case-related, verbal, adjectival, comparative, cumulative and metonymical. 

            In verbal ellipsis a verb-root joins a noun to form a compound word. 

            In comparative ellipsis the preceding word comprises the comparison and the succeeding word refers to the compared. 

             Adjectival ellipsis comprises five kinds; these have reference to quantity, form, colour, taste and quality. 

             Cumulative ellipsis is of six kinds; these refer to (1) two words, (2) many words, (3) measures, (4) weights, (5) numbers of impersonal classes, and (6) [numbers] of personal classes. (7) 

 

Loss of letters/syllables/words in the six kinds of ellipses; the signification of compounds

 

51.       In metonymical ellipsis and other ellipses sometimes the last or the middle word gets omitted. In some instances syllables and letters too get omitted. 

            The signification of each compound is indicated by the first or the second word, or by both (in the compound) or else by the other words (suppressed at the end). (8)

The Section on Prosody

 

Twofold metreme and twofold metrical foot

 

107&108. A short letter, the same followed by a consonant, a long vowel and the same followed by a consonant – all there four are called {i}nēr{/i} metreme.              {i}Karu-viḷam, kū-viḷam, tē-mā{/i} and {i}puḷi-mā{/i} are symbolic expressions of {i}mutaṟ-cīr{/i}. If they end with {i}kāy{/i} and {i}kaṉi{/i}, they are respectively symbolic expressions of {i}iṭi-c-cīr{/i} and {i}kaṭi-c-cīr{/i}. (1), (2) 

 

Fourfold metrical line

 

109.     Lines made up of 2 metrical feet, 3, 4, 5 and 6 and above are called respectively the sky, the air, the fire, the water and the zenith. They are also named, by some prosodist, {i}kuṟaḷ, cintu, aḷava, meṭil{/i} and {i}kaḷi-meṭil{/i} respectively which are dwarf line, short line, standard line, long line and overlong line. (3)

Rhyme and its kinds

 

111.     In each line of a verse, if the second letters of the first feet are the same, the first letters being of the same unit of sound ({i}māttiri{/i}), rhyme occurs there. It is of various kinds, namely

            a) rhyme where in the metrical feet the third letters being the same,

            b) rhyme taking place in the first feet of every two lines, 

            c) rhyme where the entire feet excepting the first letters are the same, 

            d) rhyme where the second letters of the rhyming feet are consonants which though not the same belong to the kindred of sounds, that of nasal and that of liquids, 

            e) rhyme where in the rhyming feet the second letters are the same consonant though animated by different vowels. 

            f) rhyme where in the rhyming feet the second letters are the same vowel though animating different consonants, and 

            g) rhyme where the consonants {i}y r l{/i} or {i}ḻ{/i} intervene between the first long letter and the second metreme without hindrance (thereto). (5) 

 

Pattiyam and kattiyam 

 

112.             Poetical composition is of two kinds, namely, {i}pattiyam{/i} and {i}kattiyam{/i}. {i}Padyam{/i} takes place by means of {i}pādam{/i} – metrical lines. {i}Kadyam{/i} is of two kinds which are {i}kaṭṭuri-pōli{/i} and {i}ceyyuṭ-pōli{/i} (those that resemble prose and those that resemble poetry). (6) 

 

Veṉpā and its kinds

 

113&114. In Veṉpā only {i}mutaṟ-cīr{/i} and {i}iṭi-c-cīr{/i} take place. The last metreme of the former will be agreeable to the first metreme of the succeeding metrical foot while that of the latter won’t be so. The last line of Veṉpā is of three feet and all the other three lines are of four feet. The last metrical foot of the last line ends as either single syllable – {i}nēr{/i} and {i}niri{/i} or as {i}nēr{/i}, two syllables – {i}nēr-nēr{/i} and {i}niri-nēr{/i} with {i}u{/i}-ending, where symbolic expressions of the four are respectively {i}nāḷ, malar, kācu{/i} and {i}piṟappam{/i}. 

            The Veṉpā occurring in 2 lines is called Kuṟaḷ-veṉpā, that occurring in 3 lines Cintiyal-veṉpā, that occurring in 4 lines Nērici-veṉpā, that occurring in more than 4 lines Pakuṟoṭi-veṉpā, that occurring as Nērici-veṉpā having more lines Kali-veṉpā that occurring in 4 lines contrary to Nērici-veṉpā, Iṉṉici-veṉpā and (7), (8) 

 

Kinds of Āciriyam

 

115.     The metre of Āciriyam in which the penultimate line also occurs with three metrical feet while all the other lines occur with four metrical feet is called Nērici-āciriyam, that where the first line and the last are of four feet while the mid-lines have two or three (or five) metrical feet is called Iṉikkuṟaḷ-āciriyam, that where all the lines occur with four metrical feet is called Vili-maṉṭilam and that where every line has a complete sense and any line may be transposed in the beginning, in the middle or at the end as per one's will, without injury to the metre or sense is called Aṭi-māṟi- maṉṭila-āciriyappā. (9) 

 

Kinds of Kali

 

116.     Kali-metre has all the six constituent members, namely Taravu, Tāḻici (which comprises 3 stanzas), Arākam, Ampōtaraṅkam detached word and Curitakam. It is of various kinds called Nērici-ottāḻici-k-kalippā, Ampōtaraṅka-ottaḻici-k-kalippā, Vaṉṉaka-ottaḻici-k-kalippā, Koccaka-k-kalippā and Veṇ-kalippā. (10) 

 

Occurrence of Vaṉṉaka-ottāḻici-k-kalippā, etc.

 

117.             Vaṉṉaka-ottāḻici-k-kalippā occurs with all the six constituent members such as Taravu and Tāḻici; Ampōtaraṅka-ottāḻici-k-kalippā occurs with the remining five members excepting Arākam; barring Arākam and Ampōtaraṅkam with the remining four members takes place Nērici-ottāḷici-k-kalippā. Veṇ-kalippā ends with a three foot line while all other lines are of four metrical feet each. (11) 

 

Kinds of Koccaka-k-kali

 

118.             Koccaka-k-kalippā is classified into five various kinds namely, Koccaka-k-kalippā occurring with one {i}Taravu{/i}, Koccaka-k-kalippā occurring with a pir of Taravu Koccaka-k-kalippā occurring with a few number of Tāḻici verses, Koccaka-k-kalippā occurring with a number of Tālici verses and Koccaka-k-kalippā in which all the constituent members occur irregularly and mixed, deviating from their rules with less or more metrical quantities than required. (12) 

 

The metres of Vañci and Maruṭipā

 

119.     Vañci metre occurs with lines of 2 feet or 3 feet in the beginning (which are Vañci lines) and ends with Curitakam-āciriyam lines, prececed by a detached word. Manuṭ-pā is a verse which begins with Venpā lines and ends with Āciriyam lines. (13) 

 

The occurrence of {i}kūṉ{/i}; Veṉ-centuṟi and Kuṟaṭṭāḻici

 

120.             {i}Kūṉ{/i} is a word which stands at the beginning of a metrical line, with sense. It may even occur in the middle and end of a line of Vañci metre. Veṉ-centuṟi occurs in 2 lines each of equal metrical quantity which is mostly 4 metrical feet. Veṉ-centuṟi having no sublime theme and flowing rhythm, Kuṟaḷ-veṉpā deviating from its Ceppal rhythm and a verse of lines with a number of metrical feet, the second line having less number of feet – all these three types are termed Kuṟaḷ-tāḻici. (14) 

 

Veḷi-viruttam, Veṉṭāḻici and Veṉṭuṟi

 

121.     Veḷi-viruttam occurs in 3 or 4 lines having the same detached metrical foot at the end of every line. If Cintiyal-veṉpā gets deviated from its rhythm of Ceppal, it becomes Veṉṭāḻici. It is Veṉṭuṟi when Nērici-veṉpā fails in its Ceppal rhythm. Veṉṭuṟi has also lines raging between 3 and 7 having less number of metrical feet in some lines of the latter half. (15) 

 

Āciriya-t-tāḻici, Āciriya-t-tuṟi and Āciriya-viruttam

 

122.     A verse having 3 lines with equal metrical quantity is called Āciriya-t-tāḻici which may occur either singly or as three stanzas on one theme. Āciriya-t-tuṟi occurs as verse having 4 lines where the penultimate is deficient (by one foot), where the second line repeats as the third and where the lines are deficient alternately. There are occasions where the mid-lines have two more metrical feet each and two less metrical feet each, where the first line has less number of feet and where the lines are overlong (having 6 and more metrical feet). Āciriya-viruttam is a verse which has 4 lines each having 6 metrical feet or more. (16.) 

 

Kali-t-tāḻici, Kali-viruttam and Kali-t-tuṟi

 

123.     Kali-t-tāḻici is a verse having 2 or more lines in which the last line sounds longer with more number of metrical feet. This may even occur as 3 verses on one theme.   Kali-viruttam is a verse of 4 lines having 4 metrical feet each. Kali-t-tuṟi occurs with 4 lines each of 5 metrical feet. (17) 

 

Kōvi-k-kali-t-tuṟi

 

124.     Kōvi-k-kali-t-tuṟi is a verse of 4 lines each of five metrical feet. If a line begins with {i}nēr{/i}, it has 16 letters and if {i}niri{/i} is its beginning metreme it has 17 letters. (The first 4 metrical feet should not deviate from Veṉṭaḷi. While reckoning the letters, consonants and the guttural are ignored. (18) 

 

Vañci-t-tuṟi, Vañci-viruttan and Vañci-t-tāḻici

 

125.     A verse occurring with two-foot lines is called Vañci-t-ta. Vañci-viruttam is a verse of lines each having three metrical feet. It is named Vañci-t-tāḻici, if Vañci-t-tuṟi occurs as three stanzas on one theme. 

Other verses also may be included in all these three various verses – Tāḻici, Tuṟi and Viruttam considering their similar aspects (alone). (19)

Sevenfold Pattiyam

 

126.             Pattiyam – poetical composition – is sevenfold, namely Kuṟaḷ, Cintu, Tiripāti, Veṉpā, Tilatam, Virattam and Cavali. If these seven kinds deviate from their rules and occur with more or less metrical quantities they are termed similitudes of the corresponding verse which are Kuṟaṭ-pōti, Cintu-p-pōli, Tiripātī-p-pōli, Veṉpā-p-pōli, Tilata-p-pōli, Viratta pōli and Cavali-p-pōli. (20) 

 

The first four defined

 

127.     Kuṟaḷ has 7 metrical feet in two lines. Cintu occurs in two lines each having equal number (mostly four) of metrical feet. Tiripāti is met with in three lines of equal metrical quantity each. Veṉpā takes place in four lines comprising 15 metrical feet in all, with one detached foot at the end of the second line (rhyming with first feet of the first two lines). (21) 

 

 Tilatam defined

 

128.             Tilatam is a four-line verse, each line comprising five metrical feet. The line beginning with {i}nēr{/i} has 16 letters while that beginning with {i}niri{/i} has 17. [The subtle difference lies between Kōvi-k-kali-t-taṟi and Tilata-k-kali-t-tuṟi (as per some antique foot-rule notes on the old commentary) in the formation of the fourth line where the third foot in Kōvi-k-kali-t-tuṟi occurs in disregard to speech rhythm while it is not so in the case of Tilata-k-kali-totuṟi. e.g. poem “{i}kaṭikamaḻ kōti nal lāy paṇṇu kōvik kalittuṟiyē{/i}” – as per Kōvi. “{i}kaṭikamaḻ kōtik kayaṉeṭuṅ kaṇṇi kalittuṟiyē{/i}” ­– as per Tilatam. (22) 

 

Viruttam defined

 

129.     A verse with four lines each comprising four metrical feet is called Viruttam. (23) 

 

Cavali and Cavali-p-pōli

 

130.             Cavali is a four-line verse where the first line, the last line or the middle lines have either more metrical fees or less (than those in other lines). If the first line is deficient it is called Mutaṟ-cavali. It is termed Iṭi-c-cavali and Kaṭi-c-cavali respectively if the mid-line or the last line of the verse is so deficient. Cavali-p-pōli is a verse which occurs in more than four lines which are of the same metrical quantity or unlike with varying number of feet in every line. (24) 

 

Sanskrit Viruttam and Taṇṭakam

 

131.     Some prosodists say that Viruttam occurs even from one letter per line (up to the maximum of 26). Unless a line comprises the mimimum of 4 letters, rhythm won't be apparent. The maximum number of letters for a line of Viruttam is 26. Those that occur with more than 26 letters per line are termed Virutta-p-pōli (similitude of Viruttam). Taṉṭakam occurs with a minimum of four Kattaḷis in the verse. (25) 

 

{i}Kuru{/i} and {i}ilaku{/i} defined.

A single short letter is {i}ilaku{/i} which has one {i}alaku{/i} and bears a symbol of a perpendicular line drawn downwards [|]. The {i}ilaku{/i} at the end of a line probably becomes a {i}kuru{/i}.  Naṭṭam defined

 

135.             Naṭṭam is the method of finding out letters (of the line) of a particular {i}cantam{/i} standing in a particular order as per {i}pirattāram{/i}. e.g.

The same process follows:  

 

[(5+1) ÷2; 6÷2] 3 is also odd. This is also {i}kuru{/i}. 

 

              [(3+1)÷2; 4÷2] 2 is even. This is {i}ilaku{/i}. 

 

[2÷2] 1 is odd.  This is {i}kuru{/i}.

Total No. of particular Cantam; the space occupied when written on ground.

 

139. A letter is supposed to occupy the space of one finger-square, (that is, one letter's length and width are the length of one finger each.)  (33) 

 

Taṇṭakam defined

 

140&141. The five symbols are {i}niri{/i} metreme for a long letter, {i}nēr{/i} metreme for a short letter, dot for a hard consonant, circle for a soft consonant and{i} vilaku{/i} for a medial consonant. 

            With the above symbols a Kattaḷi fragment ({i}tuṇṭam{/i}) is noted. With such {i}tuṇṭam{/i} numbering 4, 8, 16 and 32, a Taṇṭakam is completed. There is scarcely a symbol where soft and medical consonants are not pronounced. (34), (35) 

 

Twenty Vaṉṉams

 

142.     The author enumerates twenty Vaṇṇams which are rhythmic variations as pointed out by Tolkāppiyaṉār in Ceyyuḷiyal. Of course the order is irregular, may be due to composition of {i}kāriki{/i}. (36) 

 

The Section on Figures

 

Topic of this chapter

 

143.     The author Daṇṭi in his treatise Kāvyadarśam explins 35 figures. This author says that he follows Daṇṭi in this chapter. 

            Poetry is compared to a lady decorated with apparel and ornaments. The words are the members of her body. The subject-matter is her life. The rhythmic variation (Vaṇṇam) is her complexion. The style of language is her mode of git. (1) 

 

Defectless words are faultless members.

 

144.     The words used in poetry should signify explicitly. They must be Tamil words. If Sanskrit words are to be employed they should be Tamilised with due Tamil sound. They should have been used in verses composed by the great and learned of the past. There should be no ambiguity in the meaning. Only such defectless words are the members of the body of poetry. (2) 

 

Defective words are faulty members.

 

145.     Such words that are used in unrefined speech, that do not possess melodious sound, that are having Sanskrit letters in them and that do not signify clearly without a say of ambiguity are defective words and so faulty members of the body of poetry. (3) 

 

Clear subject-matter is defectless life.

 

146.     The subject-matter should rejoice the listener or the reader of the poetry. It should be informative profitable. It should be devoid of such defects as pointed out by scholars. It should be easily comprehensible. Such subject matter alone is deemed as defectless life of poetry. (4) 

 

Defective subject-matter is defective life.

 

147.     The subject-matter that is not easily comprehensible to the reader or the listener of the poetry, that may be argued as defective and confusing and that gives room to controversial meanings and such others is deemed as defective life of poetry. (5) 

 

The subject-matter is the life of poetry. 

 

148.     The words of poetry as well as its subject-matter should be flawless when only poetry will have figures. If they are otherwise poetry will prove lifeless. The life of poetry abides in its subject-matter. Thus spoke the author, Daṇṭi. (6) 

 

The tenfold life mentioned by the Vaitaruppar and Kauṭar

 

149.     The {i}cilīṭṭam{/i}, the {i}utārati{/i}, the {i}pulaṉ{/i}, the {i}camati{/i}, the {i}camāti{/i}, the {i}poruḷ-teḷivu{/i}, the {i}ōcam{/i}, the {i}cukumārati{/i} and the {i}iṉpam{/i} are the inherent excellence of style in the poetic composition. These are called by the {i}vitaruppar{/i} tenfold life of poetry. 

 

The {i}kuṭar{/i} have controversial explanations for all these ten merits. (7) 

 

Cilīṭṭam, etc. defined.

 

150&151.  

            1. {i}cilīṭṭam{/i} – The compactness of the metrical feet. 

            2. {i}ōcam{/i} – Omission as of an inflectional sign in combination of words. 

            3. {i}camati{/i} – The quality of having equal number of letters in all lines. 

            4. {i}camāti{/i} – Attribution of a deed to a person or thing other than its agent. 

            5. {i}cukumārati{/i} – Smoothness of expression attined by the use of soft consonants. 

            6. {i}kānti{/i} – Description within conventional limits satisfying the aesthetic sense. 

            7. {i}utāram{/i} – Description of liberality of a person. 

            8. {i}pulaṉ{/i} – Clarity of meaning by known words. 

            9. {i}poruḷ-teḷivu{/i} – The meaning becoming clear through the context that is understandable by means of a word or phrase in the verse. 

            10. {i}iṉpam{/i} – Sweetness of subject-matter and of style and diction. (8), (9) 

 

{i}taṉmi{/i}, {i}uvami{/i}, {i}uruvakam{/i}, {i}tīpakam{/i} and {i}mīṭci{/i}

 

152.     In this {i}kāriki{/i} five figures are listed each with short definition. 

             {i}taṉmi{/i} – This figure consists in describing an object as it is. It is fourfold, namely, the object, the species, the action and the quality. 

             {i}uvami{/i} – This figure consists in comparing one with the other. 

             {i}uruvakam{/i} – This figure consists in speaking the compared and the comparison as one without separation. 

             {i}dīvakam{/i} – This figure consists in linking a word with other words preceding or succeeding in the beginning, middle or end of a verse. 

             {i}mīṭci{/i} – In this figure a same word or a same meaning or both are repeated (thrice or more). (10) 

 

153.     In this {i}kāriki{/i} ten figures are listed: 1) {i}piṟaperuḷ-vippu{/i} 2) {i}taṭi-moḻi{/i} 3) {i}vitirēkam{/i} 4) {i}vipāvani{/i} 5) {i}curukku{/i} 6) {i}perukku{/i} 7) {i}nōkkam{/i} 8) {i}ētu{/i} 9) {i}nuṉukkam{/i} and 10) {i}ilēcu{/i}. (11) 

 

154.     In this {i}kāriki{/i} fifteen figures are listed: 1) {i}aṭivu{/i} 2) {i}makiḻcci{/i} 3) {i}cuvi{/i} 4) {i}ūkkam{/i} 5) {i}pariyāyamoḻi{/i} 6) {i}tuṉippēṟu{/i} 7) {i}uṭattam{/i} 8) {i}avanuti{/i} 9) {i}cilēṭi{/i} 10) {i}ciṟappu{/i} 11) {i}uṭanili-c-col{/i} 12) {i}muraṉ{/i} 13) {i}nuvalā-c-col{/i} 14) {i}terivil-pukaḻcci{/i} and 15) {i}cuṭṭu{/i}. (12) 

 

155.     In this {i}kāriki{/i}, among thirtyfive figures mentioned by Daṇṭi, the remining five are listed: 1) {i}oruṅkiyal{/i} 2) {i}parimāṟṟu{/i} 3) {i}āci{/i} 4) {i}virāvu{/i} and {i}pāvikam{/i}. There are authors who elaborate figures into many more. But this author follows only Daṇṭi. (13) 

 

156&157. These two {i}kārikis{/i} give a list of thirtytwo kinds of {i}uvami{/i}. The commentary explins them with illustrative examples. (14), (15) 

 

The general definition of {i}uvami{/i} 

 

158.     In a simile which is {i}uvami{/i} an object is compared to another either in the natural state or in prising or in deriding. The simile has four divisions, namely, the object of comparison, the particle of comparison, the object compared and the point of comparison between them both. 

            The comparison takes place consequent on quality, result, action, form, etc. (16) 

 

159.             Twelve, other kinds of simile are listed in this {i}kāriki{/i}. (17) 

 

160&161. Twenty one kinds of {i}uruvakam{/i} are listed in this {i}kāriki{/i}. (18), (19) 

 

{i}Piṟaporuḷ-vippu-aṇi{/i}

 

162.     This is a figure in which a particular notion is substanciated by a general notion and vice versa. This is of eight kinds. (20) 

 

{i}Taṭi-moḻi-aṇi

{/i} 

163&164 This is a figure in which a statement is heightened in effect by the suggestion of an apparent contradiction. This is of thirty kinds. (21), (22) 

 

{i}Vitirika-aṇi

{/i} 

165.     This is a figure in which the difference between the object of comparison and the object compared is mentioned either explicitly or suggestively. (23) 

 

{i}Vipāvaṉi-aṇi{/i} and {i}curukku-aṇi{/i}

 

166.             V{i}ipāvaṉi{/i} is a figure in which the effects are represented as taking place although their usual cuses are absent. It is of four kinds. 

             {i}Curukku{/i} is a figure in which the idea of a subject-matter is sought to be conveyed suggestively by the description of another subject-matter resembling it. It is of four kinds. (24) 

 

{i}Perukku-aṇi{/i} and {i}nōkkam-aṇi{/i}

 

167.             {i}Perukku{/i} is hyperbole. It is of five kinds. 

             {i}Nōkkam{/i} is a figure which consists in the qualities and actions of an object being ascribed to another which is entirely different in nature. It is of two kinds. (25) 

 

{i}Ētu-aṇi{/i}

 

168.             {i}Ētu{/i} is a figure of speech which brings together the cuse and effect. It is of ten kinds. (26) 

 

{i}Nuṉukkam-aṇi{/i} and {i}ilēca-aṇi{/i}

 

169.             {i}Nuṉukkam{/i} is a figure which expresses an idea by implication. It is of two kinds. 

            {i}Ilēca{/i} is a figure in which the physical expressions of an emotion in one's mind is deliberately attributed to some quite different cuse or purpose, to conceal the emotion altogether. It is also defined as a figure in which what on all hands is considered as an advantage is represented; with a deprecatory air, as a disadvantage and vice versa. In other words, it consists in prising in the disguise of degradation and deriding in the disguise of prise. It is also of two kinds. (27) 

             

 

 {i}Niral-niri, makiḻcci, cuvi, ūkkam{/i} and {i}pariyāyam{/i}

 

170.             {i}Niral-niri{/i} is also called {i}aṭivu-aṇi{/i}. It is a figure in which words or phrases are arranged in different sets so that each term of one set may qualify or govern the corresponding term in the other. 

            {i}Makiḻcci{/i} is a figure in which impassioned language is employed to give vent to one's intensive love. 

            {i}Cuvi{/i} is a figure which consists in describing the eightfold sentiment as well as the calmness of mind. Therefore it is of nine kinds. 

            {i}Ūkkam{/i} is a figure in which a person prises himself. 

            {i}Pariyāyam{/i} is a figure in which one's view is not expresses by words but is cleverly suggested. (28) 

 

{i}Tuṉi-p-pēṟu-aṇi, utātta-aṇi{/i} and {i}avanuti-aṇi{/i}

 

171.             {i}Tuṉi-p-pēṟu{/i} or {i}camāyitam{/i} is a figure in which an effort is described as proceeding from something other than its natural cuse. 

            {i}Utāttam{/i} is a figure which expresses either abundance of wealth or exaltedness of thought. It is, therefore, of two kinds. 

            {i}Avanuti{/i} is a figure which denies to an object one of its own attributes and ascribes another which is foreign to it. 

            It is of four kinds. (29) 

 

{i}Cileṭi-aṇi{/i}

 

172.             {i}Cileṭi{/i} which is paronomasia is a figure in which a single word or compound admits two or more interpretations. It is of seven kinds. (30) 

 

{i}Ciṟappu-aṇi, uṭanili-aṇi{/i} and {i}muraṉ-aṇi{/i}

 

173.             {i}Ciṟappu{/i} is a figure in which the excellence of an object is emphasised by describing it as lacking even in requisites or necessary attributes. It is of five kinds. 

             {i}Utaṉili-c-col{/i} is a figure in which an object that has to be commended for its merit or condemned for its demerit is mentioned along with several objects having the similar excellence or baseness, as the case may be, so as to render the comparison effective and telling. It is, therefore, of two kinds. 

            {i}Muraṉ{/i} is a figure in which antithesis or opposition in words and sense (in attributes and the attributed) occurs. It is therefore of three kinds. (31) 

 

 {i}Nuvalā-c-col-aṇi, terivil-pukaḻcci-aṇi{/i} and {i}cuṭṭu-aṇi{/i}

 

174.             {i}Nuvalā-c-col{/i} is a figure of speech which consists in formally prising a person or thing implying thereby disparagement of another person or thing. 

            {i}Terivil-pukaḻcci{/i} is a figure contining prise couched in words of apparent censure. {i}Cuṭṭu{/i} is a figure in which the quality or action of an object is described as affecting another, either for good or evil. It is therefore of two kinds. (32) 

 

{i}Oruṅkiyal-aṇi, parimāṟṟa-aṇi{/i} and {i}Añci-aṇi{/i}

 

175.             {i}Oruṅkiyal{/i} is a figure in which two subjects are referred to one common predicate of action or attribute. It is therefore, two kinds. 

             {i}Parimāṟṟam{/i} is a figure describing an exchange.

             {i}Añci{/i} is a figure expressing benediction. (33) 

 

{i}Viravu-aṇi{/i} and {i}pāvikam-aṇi{/i}

 

176.             {i}Viravu{/i} is a composite figure where several figures are blended (with equal merit). 

             {i}Pāvikam{/i} is the basic ethics running through a poem (34) 

 

General permissive rule regarding the figures

 

177.             Scholars elaborate the kinds in various figures which may also be taken notice of. (35) 

 

The kinds of verses

 

178.     Verses are classified into four, namely, the {i}muttakam{/i}, the {i}kuḷakam{/i}, the {i}toki-nili{/i} and the {i}kāppiyam{/i}. 

             {i}Muttakam{/i} is a stanza which by itself is complete in sense. 

             {i}Kuḷakam{/i} is the combination of two, three or more stanzas having a single finite verb in order to complete the sense; occasionally a nominal predicate is also met with.

             {i}Toki-nili{/i} is an anthology of poems collected on certin definite principles. There may be one author or many. 

             {i}Kāppiyam{/i} is the narrative poem or epic poem, may it be long or short. (36) 

 

{i}Collaṇi, Maṭakku{/i}

 

179.             {i}Collaṇi{/i} is a figure of speech depending for its effect on verbal sound alone. 

             {i}Maṭakku {/i}is the repetition of a word, foot or line in a stanza, giving a different meaning each time. It is of various kinds, namely repetition in the beginning, repetition in the end, repetition in the middle, repetition in the beginning and middle, repetition in the beginning and end, repetition in the middle and end, and repetition in all the three places. (37) 

 

The opinion of others

 

180.     The same authors are of the opinion that {i}tantira-utti{/i}, {i}tantira-kuṇam{/i}, {i}tantira-matam{/i}, {i}tantira-uri{/i}, the means of acquiring correct knowledge of an object mentioned in logic which are cuse, illustration, proof, etc. and {i}cittira-kavi{/i} are also figures of speech. You are required to know them also. 

            1. {i}Tantira-utti{/i} – The literary devices employed by an author of a standard work, who keeps in mind the rule of exegesis. They are 32 in number. 

            2. {i}Tantira-kuṇam{/i} – The beauties or excellences of a treatise being ten in number.

            3. {i}Tantira-matam{/i} – The seven kinds of attitude of an author towards a certin subject. 

            4. {i}Tantira-uri{/i} – The fourteen requisites of interpretation. 

            5.{i} Piramāṉam{/i} which is the means of acquiring correct knowledge of a thing. They are eight, namely, {i}pirattiyaṭcam{/i}, {i}aṉumāṇam{/i}, {i}ākamam{/i}, {i}uvamāṉam{/i}, {i}aruttāpatti{/i}, {i}aṉupalatti{/i}, {i}campavam{/i} and {i}aitikam{/i}. 

            6. {i}Cittira-kavi{/i} – A variety of metrical composition fitted into fanciful pictures, etc. (38) 

 

{i}Cittira-kavi 

{/i} 

181.             {i}Cittira-kavi{/i} is manifold some of which are {i}māli-māṟṟu cakkaram{/i}, {i}ōr-iṉa-p-pāṭṭu{/i}, {i}ōr-eḻuttu-p-pāṭṭu{/i}, {i}viṉā-uttaram{/i}, {i}ēka-pātam{/i}, {i}kāti-karappu{/i}, {i}cuḻikuḷam{/i}, {i}cittira-p-pā{/i} and {i}kōmūttiri{/i}. 

             {i}māli-māṟṟu{/i} – A kind of verse that remins identical where its letters are read in reverse direction. 

             {i}cakkaram{/i} – A fantastic metrical composition in the diagrammatic form of a cart-wheel. 

             {i}ōriṉappāṭṭu{/i} –  A stanza of two or more lines made of either hard consonants and hard consonant-vowels alone or soft consonants and soft consonant-vowels alone or medial consonants and medial consonant-vowels alone. 

             {i}ōreḻuttuppāṭṭu{/i} ­– A stanza of two or more lines made of a single consonant and its corresponding vowel-consonants alone. 

            {i}viṉā-uttaram{/i} –  A kind of poetical composition in which a word having a particular significance is split into a number of words, each serving as a reply to each question set before. 

            {i}ēka-pātam{/i} – A stanza of four lines all apparently alike but really made up of different sets of words and so conveying different meanings. 

             {i}kāti-karappu{/i} – A stanza so composed that by beginning with the letter of the last word reading from the end every alternate letter only, a new stanza of different metre is formed. 

            {i}cuḻikuḷam{/i} – A four-lined stanza, each line consisting of eight letters, so composed that the letters are arranged in a series of incurving loops one within another. 

             {i}cittira-p-pā{/i} – A curiously wrought poem, the composition of which is not clear for want of illustration. 

             {i}kōmūttiri{/i} – A verse of two lines composed in such a way that the first letter of the first line, the second letter of the second line, the third letter of the first line, the fourth letter of the second line and so on when read together form the first line. The first letter of the second line, the second letter of the first line, the third letter of the second line, the fourth letter of the first line and so on form the second line. 

             Certin other kinds of {i}cittira-kavi{/i} are explined in the commentary. 

            They are {i}eḻukūṟṟirukki{/i}, {i}kārantuṟi-pāṭṭu{/i}, {i}tūcaṅ-koḷal{/i},  {i}vāval-ñāṟṟu{/i}, {i}pāta-mayakku{/i}, {i}oṟṟu-p-peyarttal{/i}, {i}caruppato-pattiram{/i}, etc. (39)[5]

 

Virāviyal and Maṇippiravāḷam, etc. 

 

182.     If the Sanskrit letters mingle in the words and phrases of Tamil, that type of (poetical) composition is called Virāviyal. If Sanskrit words mingle in the sentences of Tamil, that way of composition is called Maṇi-p-piravāḷam. Tuṟi-k-kavi are verses on themes connected with Akam and Puṟam. Piraḷiki are verses on riddle. (40) 

 

{i}Tuṟi-k-kavi, kiḷavi-k-kavi{/i} and the uses of poetry

 

183.             {i}Maṟam, kaḷi, tūtu, vayirapam, campiratam, tavacu kuṟam, kaṇikam{/i}, etc. are the themes of {i}kāmpakam{/i}, a kind of poem. They are called {i}tuṟi-k-kavi{/i}. {i}Kiḷavi-k-kavi{/i} are the themes found in amatory compositions. The uses of poetry are performing virtuous deeds, earning wealth, enjoying worldly pleasures and attining salvation at the end. (41) 

 

Vīracōḻiyam ends. 

 

The {i}kāriki{/i} about the commentator.

 

The grammar work Vīracōḻiyam is a composition of 181 {i}kāriki{/i} verses by the chief of Poṉpaṟṟi, gint with many tanks and groves. In order to help those who study Tamil with intention, Peruntēvaṉ has written paraphrase with explanations to all the 181 {i}kāriki{/i}, taking it his duty. His commentary has the worth to be prised by the world.
