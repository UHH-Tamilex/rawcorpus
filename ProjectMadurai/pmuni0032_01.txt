  Kalevala the national epic of Finland: An introduction [65]*
       By Asko Parpola (University of Helsinki)
       Kalevala and the ancient Finnish folk poetry
       Kalevala, the Finnish national epic and one of the great heroic
       poems of world literature, took its definitive shape in 1849, but
       it is directly based on oral poetry that mainly came into being
       during the first millennium of the Christian era. The work was
       compiled by the great philologist Elias Lonnrot (1802-1884) from
       the best and most complete variants of ancient folk songs that he
       himself and other pioneers of Finnish folklore had collected in the
       backwoods of Karelia.
       Karelia, a vast area now mainly on the Russian side of Finland's
       eastern border, formed a periphery of Finnish-Karelian culture that
       was cut off from the centres of civilization by long distances and
       scarcely inhabited forests. The old folk poetry was preserved there
       in an oral tradition until the nineteenth and even twentieth
       century, because the Orthodox Church prevailing in Russia was more
       tolerant than the Roman Catholic Church current in other parts of
       Finland until the reformation and the Lutheran Christianity, which
       after the reformation systematically worked for the eradication of
       pagan traditions in Finland. While Kalevala on the whole reflects
       the pre-Christian religion and heroic legends of the
       Finnish-speaking peoples, its last canto has as its theme the
       triumph of Christianity, which the Swedish conquerors forced onto
       Finland in AD 1155.
       Mikeal Agricola's translation of New Testament, printed in 1548, is
       the oldest directly surviving book in the Finnish language. Very
       short samples of the closely related Karelian language are three
       centuries older; they consist of spells written on birchbark, found
       near the Russian city of Novgorod. The folk poems, though collected
       only in the nineteenth century, largely go back to pre-Christian
       times, come from widely different areas and comprise a great bulk
       of material; they are thus, in effect, among the most important
       monuments relating to the early stages of the Finnish language and
       religion.
       Comparative linguistics has shown Finnish to be an offshoot of the
       Uralic language family, whose speakers nowadays number in all about
       twenty million (the principal languages being Hungarian, Finnish
       and Estonian, with fourteen, five and one million speakers
       respectively; the other languages are spoken by small minorities in
       Russia). I shall briefly discuss this linguistic background of
       Kalevala also because there are interesting connections with Indian
       languages.
       Genetic relationship between the Uralic and Dravidian languages?
       Speakers of the Proto-Uralic language livied around 6000-4000 BC as
       hunters and fishermen in the forest regions of the northeastern
       Europe, on both sides of Ural mountains. While the Samoyeds of
       Siberia represent the eastern branch of the Uralic language family,
       the Proto-Finno-Ugrians, who lived about 4000-2500 BC in central
       Russia, form its western branch.
       The Uralic language family (or rather its Finno-Ugric branch) has
       repeatedly been mentioned among the most distant relatives of the
       Dravidian language family. Indeed, several among the most famous
       Dravidologists, such as Robert Caldwell (who spoke of Scythian
       languages), Thomas Burrow (1943-46) and M.S.Andronov, have been
       supporters of this hypothesis. Being a Finnish student of
       Dravidian, I naturally took an interest in this question, and years
       ago accumulated a lot of material for a book on the subject. I
       never finished the work, however, because I became convinced of the
       impossibility of proving by linguistics means any genetic
       relationship as distant as it is in this case assumed to be.
       Genetic affinity can be proved only by means of establishing
       regular phonetic correspondences between the two protolanguages,
       and to be able to do this in a convincing manner that allows some
       crosschecking, one needs atleast about one hundred good etyma. In
       other words, to start with, one needs minimally a hundred word
       pairs that on both sides can be reconstructed for the
       protolanguages and in which the respective reconstruction resemble
       each other reasonably closely both phonetically and semantically.
       As the reconstruction of the Uralic family itself is based on only
       about 140 accepted etymologists shared by both Proto-Finno-Urgic
       and Proto-Samoyedic (Janhunen 1981), one cannot a priori expect the
       number of etyma surviving from an even older hypothetic
       Uralo-Dravidian phylum to be much more than around 20, which would
       be much too few to prove the hypothesis. This does not, of course,
       disprove the hypothesis - after all, it is possible that all
       languages are ultimately related: L.L.Cavalli-Sforza has recently
       compared their tentative family tree to the spread of human
       populations around the globe as deduced by biology.
       (One paragraph omitted here due to difficulties in typing
       characters with special diacritical markers.)
       In addition, the Proto-Uralic and Proto-Dravidian resemble each
       other typologically in a number of features, such as the
       agglutinative morphology and the relatively simple syllable
       structure (no initial consonant clusters). Even some grammatical
       morphemes are similar: *n as the possessive (U) or oblique case
       (Dr) marker, or *i as a past tense marker (on the Uralic side in
       Proto-Finno-Ugric only: the Samoyedic verbal suffix *y does not
       seem to mark any tense).
       Ancient contacts between Finno-Ugrians and early Aryans
       The Proto-Finno-Ugrians, who lived about 4000-2500 BC in the
       forests of central Russia, had as their neighbours in the steppes
       of southern Russia the speakers of the Proto-Indo-European language
       (c. 4500-2800 BC), and then the speakers of one of its daughter
       languages, the Proto-Aryans (c. 2800-2000), whose descendents
       brought Sanskrit to India soon after 2000 BC. (Parpola in press.)
       The Finnish language preserves some ancient Aryan loanwords, such
       as 'sata' `hundred`, nearly identical with the corresponding
       Sanskrit word sata `hundred`. It is possible that even the early
       Finnish religion was influenced by Aryan ideas. Thus the principal
       Finnish word for `god`, jumala, may come from the ancient Aryan
       word dyumat `shining`, which the Rgvedic hymns apply to Indra, the
       god of war and thunder. Indra occupied the highest position in the
       ancient Indo-Aryan pantheon as did Ukko, the god of thunder, among
       the ancient Finnish deities. Another example is the miraculous mill
       in Kalevala, called sampo (colour plate 2). Its standing epithet
       `having a speckled cover` suggested that the idea of this
       wonder-mill may have come from the star-speckled cosmic `mill` of
       the revolving heavens. The word sampohas a variant form, sammas,
       which means `pillar`, and this in turn suggests a relationship with
       Sanskrit skambha or stambha, which occurs in the Veda as the name
       for the cosmic pillar that upholds the sky.
       Proto-Finns and Lapps
       By the first millennium BC at the latest, the Balto-Finnic offshoot
       of the Uralic language family had reached the Baltic region and
       Finland and divided into two major branches. Proto-Lapps occupied
       the northern areas, probably most parts of Finland, while
       Proto-Finns (whose present-day linguistic descendents include the
       speakers of Estonian, Finnish and Karelian) were settled in
       Estonia, Karelia and southernmost Finland. The poems of Kalevala
       belong to the Proto-Finnish branch. The Proto-Finns were engaged in
       primitive farming and had a more advanced material culture, which
       they had adopted - along with loanwords and ideas - from the
       neighbouring Indo-European-speaking peoples, including Germanic
       (Nordic or Scandinavian), Baltic (Latvian and Lithuanian) and
       Slavic (Russian) tribes. The songs of Kalevala reflect the
       northward expansion of the Proto-Finns and their hostility towards
       the Lapps, their linguistic relatives, who nowadays survive as a
       small minority in the northernmost fringes of Scandinavia near the
       Artic Ocean. Traditionally the Lapps have been hunters and nomadic
       reindeer herders. It is probably the Lapps that the Roman author
       Tacitus meant in AD 98. When in his description of the northernmost
       borders of Europe he spoke of a people called Fenni who lived by
       hunting and food-gathering, with no permanent homes.
       Contents of Kelavala
       The war expeditions of Kalevala, which also have as their
       historical background the Viking raids of the late first millennium
       A.D., form the backbone of the poems, as does war between the
       Paandavas and the Kauravaas in the Mahaabhaarata, or Raama's
       expedition to Lanka in the Raamaayana. But the poems are by no
       means limited to warring themes. Wooing with physical feats of the
       would-be-groom, is another popular heroic theme, comparable to the
       svayamvaras of the Indian epics. The Kalevala poems also deal with
       many other aspects of everyday life like agriculture (see fig. 2)
       and herding (colour plate 6), and all kinds of folk customs like
       marriage and healing rituals, or the pastimes of the youth, and, of
       course, with the world view and religion. One gets a fairly
       comprehensive description of the ancient Finnish culture, somewhat
       in the manner that all the facets of ancient Tamil culture are
       presented in Cilappatikaaram (we must not forget, however, that the
       Finnish culture was completely rural and much simpler than the
       sophisticated and largely urban culture of the Tamils). The most
       ancient religion of the peoples speaking Uralic languages was
       probably shamanistic, but the religion reflected in Kalevala has
       been much influenced by the other peoples with whom the Baltic
       Finns had been in contact. In fact there are many world views in
       Kalevala: myths going back to the Stone Age, like the poems of
       creation and the felling of the grat oak (discussed below), legends
       connected with the adventurers of the Viking Age and with
       Chirstianity, and the poems by farmers, shamans and women.
       The great oak and its felling
       As an illustration of Kalevala's contents I would like to discuss
       in some detail the second song, which follows upon the myth of the
       world's creation in the first song. The wise sage Va*ina*mo*inen
       wants to have trees sown upon the bare islands and mainland that
       rise from the primeval ocean. This is done by the spirit guarding
       the fertility of the soil, Sampsa Pellervoinen. The seeds of all
       trees flourish excepting that of the oak. Only after the demigods
       of the sea burn some hay and an acorn falls on these ashes, does
       the oak start to grow. But it grows up to the sky, into an enormous
       tree that prevents the sun and the moon from shining. The whole
       world is doomed to darkness. Va*ina*mo*inen looks in vain for a
       feller for the giant tree, and finally prays to his mother, the
       maiden of the sea. She sends a tiny man, of the length of a thumb,
       whose appearance makes Va*ina*mo*inen laugh and ridicule him,
       rather like the demon Bali who was misled by the looks of God Visnu
       in his dwarf incarnation. Just like Vamana, howver, the little
       coppery man (undoubtedly a personification of the ruddy rising
       morning sun) suddenly grows into an enormous size and fells the
       terrible tree by hitting it three times with his axe. Nature is now
       freed from the evil of darkness and starts to blossom. (One is
       reminded of the relief of the world when god Indra destroyed the
       demon 'Vrtra' and released all the waters that this monster had
       been retaining.) Splinters of the fallen oak scatter happiness
       throughout the world. This last mentioned motif seems to be
       connected with rituals of brewing beer around the central oak in
       the sacred grove, and of making beer cups out of oak wood, which
       had magic powers. The rest of the song describes swidden
       agriculture, the normal method of cultivation in ancient times,
       which is no longer in use (in fact it is prohibited): it invloves
       cutting all trees in a large area and burning them before the seed
       is sown in the ashes. (Fig. 2.) Va*ina*mo*inen saves, however, one
       birch tree for the cuckoo bird to sit on.
       Kalevala
       The name Kalevala ends in the Finnish place name suffix -la. The
       remining stem Kaleva has been understood as the proper name of the
       most ancient ancestor of the Finns, who had twelve sons, among them
       such heroes of Kalevala as Vainamoinen and Ilmarinen. In Finnish,
       the word Kaleva occurs in several names of stars ( the belt of
       Orion is called `the sword of Kaleva`) and other heavenly phenomena
       like thunderbolts (`fires of Kaleva`), and the sons of Kaleva are
       said to have been giants who destroyed forests as superpowerful
       masters of swidden agriculture (where trees are burnt to make
       fields, fig. 2). The etymology of the name is uncertain, but the
       most likely explanation connects it with the Lithunian word
       'kalvis' `smith` and the ancient Baltic smith god Kalevias.
       Ilmarinen, the divine smith
       One of the principal heroes of Kalevala, Ilmarinen, is specifically
       a smith. Among his main feats are the taming of iron, the forging
       of the miraculous mill sampo (colour plate 2), ploughing a field
       full of vipers (colour plate 5), the making of a golden maiden and
       releasing the heavenly lights from the rock of the north. In
       ancient folk poems Ilmarinen has forged the heavenly vault as well
       as the sampo. Depicted on a shamanic drum from Lapland dating from
       1692 is an anthropomorphic god, called Ilmaris, who is said to
       regulate the air and the wind. The Finnish word ilma means `air`
       and the Votyaks living in Russia still worship a sky god called
       Inmar or Ilmar.
       Va*ina*mo*inen and the power of songs
       The main actor of Kalevala, Va*ina*mo*inen, is a many-sided figure
       with both divine and human features. Lo*nnrot seems to have
       favoured the latter at the expense of mythological characteristics.
       In the first song he is the creator god born in the primeval sea,
       and also his name, derived from va*ina* meaning `a broad, deep and
       slow-flowing river` suggets that originally he might have been a
       deity connected with waters, somewhat like Varuna in the Indian
       mythology. Va*ina*mo*inen also acts as a culture hero: he is the
       first builder of a boat and the first maker of a zither, who
       enchants the entire nature with his music. Va*ina*mo*inen's
       epithets emphasis his age and wisdom: he is a mighty sage possessed
       of supermundane knowledge, who performs his feats by means of magic
       songs and powerful words. Like a shaman, he descends to the
       netherworld to fetch the missing mantras from a dead ancient giant.
       Although Va*ina*mo*inen often appears as a warrior, his capacities
       as a fighter are not appreciated nearly as much as is his
       knowledge. It is interesting to note in this connection that the
       Finnish word for `hero`, 'sankari', goes back to the Old Nordic
       word 'sangare' `singer`. In a singing contest, Va*ina*mo*inen's
       mighty words make his adversary sink into a swamp. Different
       scholars have come forward with widely different and controversial
       results in their efforts to comprehend in simplistic terms the
       character of Va*ina*mo*inen. The same applies to many other figures
       of Kalevala. A similar situation prevails in the study of the
       Indian epics: the five Pandavas, for instance, have been
       interpreted as incarnations of various deities already in ancient
       times, while other scholars take them to be historical human
       heroes.
       Elias Lo*nnrot
       Although Kalevala is based on ancient folk songs, we must not
       forget that its compiler also has a considerable share in its
       formation. It remains the work of Elias Lo*nnrot (1802-1884), son
       of a poor villiage tailor, who started his career as a medical
       doctor, but who became a Professor of the Finnish language with as
       extraordinarily wide range of cultural activities. On elven long
       treks through largely roadless wildernesses, Lo*nnrot travelled by
       foot some twenty thousand kilometers and collected about 65,000
       lines of Kalevala-type folk songs. After a doctoral dissertation on
       the poems dealing with Va*ina*mo*inen (1822), he published the
       first edition of Kalevala, the so calleed "Old Kalevala", in 1835;
       the second and final edition, nearly twice the length of the first,
       comprising 50 songs and 22,795 lines in all, came out in 1849. An
       anthology of lyric folk poetry called Kanteletar was published by
       Lo*nnrot in 1840-41. The original sources of these publications
       still exist, and afford a clear idea of its relationship to the
       genuine folklore. Lonnrot gave Kalevala its overall shape and
       structure, and in linking the various originally independent
       episodes composed himself about 600 lines which have no parallel in
       the folk material.
       A large body of the original folk poetry on which Kalevala and
       Kanteletar are based has been published in 33 thick volumes of
       Suomen kansan vanhat runot (`The ancient poems of the Finnish
       people`) in 1908-1948. Even this huge work does not exhaust the
       rich collections that hundreds of scholars and volunteer collectors
       of folklore have accumulated in the archives of the Finnish
       Literature Society. Established in 1831, the Finnish Literature
       Society is one of oldest and largest collections of oral tradition
       in the world. In 1977, these collections comprised some 86,800
       songs in Kalevala metre; 129,400 rhymed folk-songs; 52,400
       incantations; 336,000 spells, beliefs and omens; 187,400 games;
       9,300 nonsense verse and laments; 96,000 fairy tales; 1,600
       religious legends; 103,200 supernatural tales and memorates; 77,800
       historical and local tales; 7,700 aetiological stories and myths;
       766,500 proverbs (complemented by 1,425,000 proverb variants in the
       collections of Helsinki University); 117,300 riddles; 23,200 folk
       tunes; and 54,000 ethnographic descriptions.
       Kalevala as the national epic of the Finns
       Lo*nnrot's work, particularly the publication of Kalevala, had an
       immerse effect on the self-awareness and national feeling of the
       Finns, who had been dominated by the Swedes from 1155 to 1809 and
       then (after a war between Sweden and Russia, which Sweden lost) by
       the Russians from 1809 to 1917, when Finland finally obtained
       independence. Lo*nnrot's service to world literature in salvaging
       the ancient heritage from the destruction can be compared with that
       rendered by the famous resurrector of the Old Tamil Cankam
       literature, Uu. Vee. Caaminaathaiyar. The famous Finnish composer
       Jean Sibelius (1865-1957) introduced many themes of Kalevala to
       world music. His music has in the hearts of the Finns a position
       comparable to the music of Thiyaagaraajar among the Tamils. The
       poems of Kalevala have greatly inspired also the greatest Finnish
       visual artists, above all Akseli Gallen-Kallela (1865-1931). The
       vignettes at the beginning and end of each poem have been taken
       from his artistic Kalevala edition, and a small selection of his
       Kalevala paintings and frescoes are reproduced in the frontispiece
       and on the eight colour plates. It is hoped that they can also
       mediate some impression of Finnish nature, still today
       characterized by lakes and forests; they render this artist's view
       of the cultural milieu that created the poems.
       The Tamil translation by Mr. R. Sivalingam
       Oxford University Press has recently published a new English
       translation of Kalevala by Keith Bosley in its series `The World's
       Classics` (1989). This translation, the earlier English renderings
       by W.F.Kirby (1907, reprinted in Gallen-Kallela 1985) and
       F.B.Magoun, Jr. (1963), together with some other English
       translations have been taken into consideration in the present
       Tamil interpretation. The translator, Mr R. Sivalingam of Jaffna,
       Sri Lanka, is an experiened writer well known to Tamil readers
       through his numerous novels and short stories published under the
       pen name Udayanan. His two novels Ponnaana malarallavoo and
       An-taranka kiitam were published by the Express Newspapers (Ceylon)
       Ltd. in Colombo in their series of `Virakesari Publications`.
       Having lived in Finland for more than ten years, he has become
       acquainted with the Finnish culture and language and has been able
       to base his rendering of Kalevala directly on the Finnish-Karelian
       original. He has carefully polished its form with the help of his
       Sri Lankan poet friend, S. Krishnapillai (`Thimilaithumilan`).
       Naturally, it has been impossible to retain all the poetic
       peculiarties of the original. The Kalevala metre consists basically
       of trochaic tetrametres (-v / -v / -v / -v ), with two such lines
       usually forming a pair: the second line repeats in different words
       the message conveyed by the first line. Instead of rhyme at the end
       of the line, there is often alliteration: two or more words within
       a line begin with the same sound. It has been possible to imitate
       this latter device in the Tamil translation. Compare the Finnish
       original and the Tamil translation of the initial verses of the
       work reproduced below:

       These initial lines contain typical stock phrases with which the
       folk singers used to introduce their performance. It was common for
       two persons to sing together, hand in hand (fig. 3), slowly rocking
       their bodies from side to side. Musical accompaniment was provided
       with a zither, called kantele (fig. 4). The Kalevala type poetry
       was sung in simple melodies known in two main varieties (fig. 5).
       Though the melodies are simple, the best singers could vary and
       repeat them in so many ways that they never seemed monotonous.
       Of course, the translation of a work reflecting a totally different
       geographic and cultural milieu from that of Tamil Nadu and Sri
       Lanka has posed many problems. Let us remember that snow and
       skiing, for example, were totally unfamiliar to the Tamils before
       the development of modern communication. And how to render the
       names of plants and berries not growing in southern Asia? All such
       problems could perhaps not be fully solved, but the Tamil reader is
       given additional information concerning proper names and
       problematic expressions (marked in the translation with single and
       double asterisks respectively) in the glossaries and explanations
       at the end of the work.
       I know Tamils to be avid readers and consumers of culture, who are
       proud to possess great epics of much the same age as the Kalevala
       poetry: the Cilappathikaaram, MaNimeekalai, Ciivakacin-thamaNi and
       Kampa-raamaayaNam. I am convinced that they will appreciate the
       service done by R. Sivalingam to the cause of Tamil culture and
       literature as he has enriched it with a direct and outstanding
       rendering of one of the great epics of the world, just as we Finns
       feel happy that our ancient heritage can now reach lovers of good
       literature on the other side of the globe. Tamil is now the 30th
       language into which Kalevala has so far been translated in its
       entirety; abbreviated translations have been made into a dozen or
       so further languages.

       vāḻnta 'muṉ-piṉṉō-ukiriyaru'kku (the Proto-Finno-Ugrians) teṉ
       raṣṣiyāviṉ camaveḷikaḷil vāḻnta 'muṉ-intō-airōppiya' (the
       Proto-Indo-European) moḻi pēciyōr ayalavarāka iruntaṉar (cumār
       4500-2800 ki.mu.); piṉṉar ataṉ vaḻivanta moḻikaḷil oṉṟaip pēciya
       'muṉ-āriyariṉ' (the Proto-Aryans) (cumār 2800-2000 ki.mu.) vaḻit
       tōṉṟalkaḷ ki.mu. 2000ai aṭuttu camaskirutattai intiyāvukkuk koṇṭu
       vantaṉar (pārpolāviṉ ākkam - accil). cila paḻaiya camaskiruta
       iraval coṟkaḷ piṉṉiṣ moḻiyil pātukākkappaṭukiṟatu; ōr
       eṭuttukkāṭṭāka, 'nūṟu' eṉṉum poruḷuṭaiya 'sata' 'cata' eṉṟa piṉṉiṣ
       col, 'sata' eṉṟa camaskirutac colluṭaṉ kiṭṭattaṭṭac cariyāka
       ottirukkiṟatu. ātiyil irunta piṉṉiṣ matam āriyak koḷḷaikaḷiṉ tākka
       viḷaivākakkūṭa iruntirukkalām. ivvāṟu 'kaṭavuḷ' eṉṉum poruḷuṭaiya
       'jumala' eṉṟa piṉṉiṣ mūlac col, irukkuvētap pāṭalkaḷil pōrukkum
       iṭimuḻakkattukkum teyvamāṉa intiraṉaik kuṟippiṭum 'pirakācittal'
       eṉṉum poruḷuṭaiya 'dyumat' eṉṟa paḻaiya āriyac collil iruntu
       vantirukkalām. intiya āriyarkaḷiṉ teyvaṅkaḷil intiraṉ uyarnta
       nilaiyaip peṟṟār; piṉṉiṣ paḻaiya teyvaṅkaḷil iṭimuḻakkattiṉ
       teyvamāṉa 'ukkō' (Ukko)vum avvāṟē karutappaṭṭār. iṉṉoru
       eṭuttukkāṭṭu kalēvalāvil varum 'campō' eṉṉum aṟputa ālaiyākum.
       cuḻalum cuvarkkattiṉ naṭcattirap puḷḷikaḷuṭaiya iyalulaka
       aṇṭattukkuriya 'ālai'yiliruntu inta aṟputa ālaikkāṉa eṇṇam
       ēṟpaṭṭirukkalām eṉṟu campōviṉ 'puḷḷikaḷuḷḷa mūṭi' eṉṟa nilaiyāṉa
       aṭaimoḻi karuta vaikkiṟatu. 'campō' (sampo) eṉṉum collil iruntu
       varum 'tūṇ' eṉṉum poruḷuḷḷa tiripuru sammas eṉpatu, skambha allatu
       stambha eṉṟa camaskirutac colliṉ toṭarpai niṉaivūṭṭukiṟatu;
       vētattil itu vāṉattait tāṅki niṟkum iyalulaka aṇṭattukkuriya tūṇaik
       kuṟikkiṟatu.
       piṉlāntiyariṉ muṉṉōrum lāppiyarum
       kiṟistuvukku muntiya mutal āyiram āṇṭup pakutiyil yūrālik moḻik
       kuṭumpattil iruntu kiḷaiviṭṭa pālṭō -piṉṉik (Balto-Finnic), pālṭik
       piratēcattaiyum piṉlāntaiyum aṭaintu iru perum kiḷaikaḷākap
       pirintatu. lāppiyariṉ muṉṉōr vaṭa pakutikaḷil, perumpālum piṉlāntiṉ
       perum pakutikaḷil taṅkiya kālattil, piṉlāntiyariṉ muṉṉōr
       estōṉiyāvilum karēliyāvilum piṉlāntiṉ teṉ pakutikaḷilum
       kuṭiyēṟiṉar; (ivarkaḷuṭaiya iṉṟaiya moḻivaḻit tōṉṟalkaḷ estōṉiyā,
       karēliyā, piṉṉiṣ moḻikaḷaip pēcupavarkaḷil aṭaṅkuvar.) kalēvalāk
       kavitaikaḷ 'muṉ-piṉṉiṣ' (Proto-Finnish) kiḷaiyaic cērntatu.
       piṉlāntiyariṉ muṉṉōr paṇṭaiya vivacāyattil īṭupaṭṭiruntatōṭu,
       jermāṉiya (nōrṭik allatu skaṉṭiṉēviya), pālṭik (latviyaṉ +
       littuvēṉiya), slāviya (raṣya) iṉattavar uṭpaṭṭa intiya airōppiya
       moḻikaḷaip pēcum makkaḷiṭamiruntu peṟṟa iraval coṟkaḷ,
       koḷkaḷiliruntu apivirutti aṭainta muṉṉēṟṟamāṉa kalācāra
       aṭippaṭaiyaiyum koṇṭiruntaṉar. kalēvalāp pāṭalkaḷ, piṉlāntiyariṉ
       muṉṉōriṉ vaṭakku nōkkiya virivākkattaiyum, lāppiyarpāl irunta
       pakaimaiyaiyum, moḻit toṭarpukaḷaiyum eṭuttukkāṭṭukiṟatu; inta
       lāppiyar ippoḻutu vaṭa camuttirattukku arukil skaṉṭiṉēviyāviṉ
       vaṭapuṟa ellaiyil oru ciṟiya ciṟupāṉmaiyiṉarākak kālam
       taḷḷukiṉṟaṉar. lāppiyar paramparai paramparaiyāka
       vēṭṭaikkārarākavum kalaimāṉ mantaikaḷai vaḷarkkum nāṭō ṭikaḷākavum
       iruntaṉar. ki. pi. 98l ṟōmaṉ nūlāciriyar ṭasiṭṭas (Tacitus)
       airōppiya vaṭapuṟa ellaikaḷaip paṟṟi viparikkaiyil vēṭṭaiyāṭi,
       uṇavukaḷ cēkarittu, nirantaramāṉa vīṭukaḷillāta 'peṉṉi' (Fenni)
       eṉṟa oru iṉattavaraip paṟṟik kūṟiyirukkiṟār; itu perumpālum inta
       lāppiyarāka irukkalām.
       kalēvalāviṉ uḷḷaṭakkam
       makāpāratattil pāṇṭavarkaḷukkum keḷaravarkaḷukkum iṭaiyē ēṟpaṭṭa
       pōriṉaip pōla allatu irāmāyaṇattil irāmar ilaṅkaikku mēṟkoṇṭa
       paṭaiyeḻucciyaip pōla, kiṟistuvukku piṟpaṭṭa mutal āyiram āṇṭup
       pakutiyil nikaḻnta skaṉṭiṉēviyak kaṭalvīrarkaḷiṉ tākkutalkaḷiṉāl
       ēṟpaṭṭa varalāṟṟup piṉṉaṇiyumuṭaiya kalēvalāviṉ pōr naṭavaṭikkaikaḷ
       ippāṭalkaḷiṉ mutukelumpāka amaintaṉa. āṉāl ekkāraṇam koṇṭum
       ippāṭalkaḷ pōrk karupporuḷai maṭṭum aṭippaṭaiyākak koṇṭavaiyalla.
       intiyāviṉ kāviyaṅkaḷil varum cuyamvaraṅkaḷaip pōla, māppiḷḷaiyākap
       pōkiṟavar peṇṇai nayantu peṟutalum makkaḷ virumpum oru kāviyak
       karuvākum. vivacāyam (paṭam 2), kālnaṭai vaittiruttal (varṇap paṭam
       6), vivākaṅkaḷ, maruttuvac caṭaṅkukaḷ pōṉṟa ellā vakaiyāṉa kirāmīya
       vaḻakkaṅkaḷ, allatu iḷam makkaḷiṉ poḻutupōkkukaḷ eṉpavaṟṟōṭu ulaka
       nōkkum matamum kūṭa uṭpaṭṭa nāḷānta vāḻkkaiyiṉ palvēṟu paṇpukaḷ
       paṟṟiyum kalēvalā kūṟukiṟatu. paṇṭaiya tamiḻariṉ kalācārattiṉ ellāp
       pakkaṅkaḷaiyum cilappatikārattil aḷittiruppatu pōla, paṇṭaiya
       piṉlāntiyariṉ kalācārattiṉ ciṟanta virivāṉa varṇaṉaiyaik
       kalēvalāvil kāṇalām. (iruppiṉum tamiḻ makkaḷuṭaiya nērtti
       nuṭpamāṉatum perumaḷavil nakarap paṇpāṉatumāṉa kalācārattilum
       pārkka, piṉlāntiyariṉ kalācāram muṟṟilum kirāmīyamāṉatum mikavum
       eḷimaiyāṉatumākum eṉpatai nām maṟantu viṭakkūṭātu.) yūrālik moḻikaḷ
       pēcum makkaḷiṉ mikap paḻaiya matam aṉēkamāka 'ṣamāṉicam'
       (Shamanism) āka iruntirukkalām; āṉāl pālṭik-piṉlāntiyar toṭarpu
       koṇṭirunta piṟa makkaḷiṉ viḷaivāṭcikku uṭpaṭṭa matamē kalēvalāvil
       piratipalikkiṟatu. uṇmaiyil, kalēvalāvil pala ulaka nōkkukaḷaik
       kāṇalām; avai ulakap paṭaippup paṟṟiya pāṭalkaḷ, periya cinturam
       (great oak) vīḻttap paṭutal (itu paṟṟik kīḻē kūṟappaṭukiṟatu),
       skaṉṭiṉēviyak kaṭalvīrarkaḷiṉ cākacac ceyalkaḷ paṟṟiyum kiṟistuvam
       paṟṟiyum paramparaik kataikaḷ, vivacāyikaḷ, mantirakkārarkaḷ,
       mantirakkārikaḷiṉ pāṭalkaḷ ākiyavai aṭaṅkiya kaṟkālam varai
       piṉnōkkic cellakkūṭiya peḷarāṇikak karuttukaḷākum.
       māperum cintūramum, atu vīḻtalum
       kalēvalāviṉ uḷḷaṭakkattukku ōr utāraṇamāka ataṉ iraṇṭāvatu pāṭalaic
       caṟṟu virivākak kūṟa virumpukiṟēṉ; mutalāvatu pāṭalil varum ulakam
       paṭaikkappaṭṭa purāṇak kataiyait toṭarntu ippāṭal varukiṟatu.
       ātiyil tōṉṟiya camuttirattil eḻunta tīvukaḷilum talainilap
       parappilum maraṅkaḷai vitaittu uṇṭākka virumpukiṟāṉ pēraṟivu
       paṭaitta ñāṉiyāṉa vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ. maṇvaḷattaik kākkum tēvacaktiyāṉa
       camsā pellarvoyiṉaṉāl itu naṭaipeṟukiṟatu. cintūra marattait tavira
       maṟṟa ellā vitaikaḷum muḷaittuc ceḻikkiṉṟaṉa. kaṭaliṉ upatēvataikaḷ
       koñca vaikkōlai erittu, ataṉ cāmparmēl cintūra marattiṉ vittu
       viḻunta piṉṉar tāṉ cintūram muḷaikkat toṭaṅkukiṟatu. āṉāl atu oru
       māperum viruṭcamāka vāṉam varai vaḷarntu cūriyaṉum cantiraṉum
       oḷirvatait taṭukkiṟatu. muḻu ulakamē iruḷil amiḻkiṟatu.
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ inta irāṭcaca marattai vīḻttak kūṭiya oruvaṉait tēṭik
       kiṭaikkāmal, kaṭaiciyil taṉatu tāyāṉa kaṭalmakaḷai vaṇaṅkukiṟāṉ.
       avaḷ peruviral aḷavu nīḷamāṉa oru ciṉṉañciṟiya maṉitaṉai
       aṉuppukiṟāḷ; viṣṇu pakavāṉ kuṟaḷ vaṭivil avatāram eṭutta pōtu pali
       eṉṉum arakkaṉ avarait tavaṟāka niṉaittataipōla, intak kuṟaḷ
       maṉitaṉaiyum pārttu vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ cirittu ēḷaṉam ceykiṟāṉ. āyiṉum,
       vāmaṉaṉaip pōla intac ciṉṉañciṟiya ceppu maṉitaṉum tiṭīreṉa māperum
       aḷavu uruvattaip peṟṟu, antap payaṅkara marattaik kōṭāriyāl
       mummuṟai tākki vīḻttukiṟāṉ; (intat tōṟṟam ceṉṉiṟamāṉa utayakkāla
       cūriyaṉiṉ uruvakamē eṉpatil cantēkamillai). iyaṟkai ippoḻutu iruḷ
       eṉṉum kēṭṭiliruntu viṭupaṭṭu malarcciyaṭaiyat toṭaṅkukiṟatu. (tēvaṉ
       intiraṉ viruttirācuraṉaik koṉṟu, inta arakkaṉ taṭuttu vaittirunta
       maḻai mukilkaḷai ellām viṭuvittu ulakattukku viṭutalai tantatai
       iṅku niṉaivu kūṟalām). vīḻnta cintūra marattiṉ tuṇṭukaḷ ulakellām
       iṉpattaip parappukiṟatu. itaṉ nōkkam mantira cakti vāynta,
       puṉitamāṉa cōlai mattiyil cintūrattaic cuṟṟi 'pīr' eṉṉum pāṉam
       vaṭikkum caṭaṅkukaḷ, cintūra marattil 'pīr'p pāṉak kiṇṇaṅkaḷ
       iyaṟṟutal pōṉṟavaṟṟait toṭarpupaṭuttuvatākat terikiṟatu. pāṭaliṉ
       ēṉaiya pakuti ātikālattil cātāraṇamākak kaiyāḷappaṭṭa kāṭukaḷai
       erittaḻittuc ceyta vivacāyam paṟṟik kūṟukiṟatu; itu ippoḻutu
       vaḻakkilillai; (cariyākac collap pōṉāl taṭaiceyyappaṭṭuḷḷatu);
       atāvatu cāmpalil vitaikaḷai vitaippataṟku muṉṉāl perum piratēcattil
       uḷḷa ellā maraṅkaḷaiyum muṟṟāka erippatu (paṭam 2). eṉiṉum, kuyil
       vantu amarvataṟkāka vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ oru milāṟu marattaip pātukākkiṟāṉ.
       kalēvā
       kalēvalā Kalevala eṉṉum peyar piṉṉiṣ moḻiyil 'iṭam' eṉpataik
       kuṟippiṭum -la eṉṉum peyar vikutiyil muṭivaṭaikiṟatu. 'kalēvā'
       eṉṉum eñciya aṭi piṉlāntiyariṉ cantatiyiṉ ātimutalvariṉ peyarākak
       karutappaṭukiṟatu. ivarukku paṉṉiraṇṭu āṇ makkaḷ iruntaṉar;
       kalēvalāviṉ nāyakarkaḷāṉa vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉum ilmariṉaṉum ivarkaḷil
       aṭaṅkuvar. piṉṉiṣ moḻiyil 'kalēvā' eṉpatu viṇmīṉkaḷiṉ pala
       peyarkaḷāka varukiṟatu; [māṉtalai viṇmīṉkuḻuvai (the belt of orion)
       'kalēvāviṉ vāḷ' eṉṟu aḻaippar.] iṭiyēṟu pōṉṟa vāṉulakak kāṭciyai
       'kalēvalāviṉ neruppu' eṉpar. kalēvāviṉ āṇ makkaḷai, vayalkaḷai
       uṇṭākkuvataṟkāka (maraṅkaḷai erittuc) cuṭṭaḻitta kāṭṭu
       vivacāyattuṟaiyiṉ aticakti vāynta pūtaṅkaḷ eṉpar. kalēvā eṉṉum
       peyariṉ collākka viḷakkam uṟutiyāka colvataṟkillai. 'kollaṉ' eṉṉum
       poruḷ varum kalvis eṉṟa littuvēṉiyaṉ collum paḻaiya pālṭik
       kolvēlait teyvam kalevias eṉpatum tāṉ toṭarpupaṭuttakkūṭiya mika
       neruṅkiya viḷakkamākum.
       ilmariṉaṉ eṉṉum tēva kollaṉ
       kalēvalāviṉ mukkiya nāyakarkaḷil oruvaṉāṉa ilmariṉaṉ kollaṉ eṉṉum
       taṉic ciṟappuṭaiyavaṉ. ivaṉuṭaiya mukkiya aruñceyalkaḷil cila:
       irumpaip paṭimāṉamākkutal, campō eṉṉum aṟputa ālaiyai ulaiyil
       uruvākkutal (varṇappaṭam 2), pāmpukaḷ niṟainta vayalai uḻutal
       (varṇappaṭam 5), taṅkattil oru maṅkaiyai iyaṟṟutal, viṇṇulaka
       oḷikaḷai vaṭanilap pāṟaikaḷiliruntu viṭuvittal eṉpaṉavām. paḻaiya
       nāṭō ṭip pāṭalkaḷiṉpaṭi, ilmariṉaṉ campōvaip pōlavē viṇṇulakiṉ
       kavikai vimāṉattaiyum ceytirukkiṟāṉ. lāppulāntiliruntu kiṭaitta
       1692m kālattaiya 'ṣamāṉisa' matakuruviṉ muraciṉpaṭi (drum) ilmaris
       eṉṉum maṉpuṉaivāṉa teyvam kāṟṟaiyum kāṟṟuvīccaiyum
       oḻuṅkicaivuppaṭuttiyatākac collappaṭukiṟatu. piṉṉiṣ moḻiyil ilma
       eṉṉum collukkuk kāṟṟu eṉṟu poruḷ. raṣṣiyāvil vāḻum 'votyāks'
       (Votyaks) iṉattavar iṉṉamum 'iṉmar' Inmar allatu 'ilmer' Ilmer
       eṉṉum viṇṇukkuriya teyvattai vaḻipaṭukiṉṟaṉar.
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉum pāṭalkaḷiṉ caktiyum
       kalēvalāviṉ mukkiya nāyakaṉāṉa vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ maṉitac ciṟappu teyvac
       ciṟappu ākiya iraṇṭum koṇṭa oru pāttiramākum. purāṇattuṟait
       taṉicciṟappukaḷiṉ aṭippaṭaiyil loṇrot (Lo*nnrot) piṉṉataṟkē
       cātakamāka irukkiṟār eṉṟu terikiṟatu. mutalāvatu pāṭalil
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉē āti kālattuk kaṭalil piṟanta paṭaippuk kaṭavuḷākiṟāṉ;
       avaṉuṭaiya peyar 'akaṉṟu āḻamāṉatum metuvākap pāyvatumāṉa āṟu'
       eṉṉum poruḷil uḷḷa va*ina* eṉṉum collil iruntu vantatāl, ātiyil
       taṇṇīrōṭu toṭarpuṭaiya kaṭavuḷākavum intiyāviṉ purāṇaṅkaḷil varum
       varuṇaṉaip pōlavum iruntirukkalām eṉṟu karutavaikkiṟatu.
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ oru kalācāra nāyakaṉākavum karutappaṭukiṟāṉ; oru
       paṭakai mutalil kaṭṭiyavaṉ avaṉē; oru yāḻai mutalil ceytu iyaṟkai
       muḻuvataiyum taṉatu icaiyāl mayakkiyavaṉum avaṉē. vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉiṉ
       paṇpai viḷakkum ciṟappup peyarkaḷ avaṉuṭaiya vayataiyum aṟivaiyum
       aḻuttik kūṟukiṉṟaṉa; avaṉ ulakiyalukku appāṟpaṭṭa aṟivu paṭaitta
       oru vallamaimikka ñāṉi; mantirap pāṭalkaḷālum cakti vāynta
       coṟkaḷālum taṉatu aruñceyalkaḷai nikaḻttupavaṉ. oru paḻaiya iṟanta
       pūtattiṭam tēvaiyāṉa mantirac coṟkaḷaip peṟuvataṟkāka oru mantira
       cūṉiya matakuruvaippōla pātāḷa ulakattukkuc celpavaṉ. vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ
       oru pōrvīraṉaip pōla aṭikkaṭi kāṭciyaḷittālum, avaṉuṭaiya
       pōrvīraṉukkuriya ceyalāṟṟal avaṉuṭaiya ñāṉattiṉ tērcciyaḷavukkup
       pārāṭṭappaṭavillai. itaṉ toṭarpāka, nāyakaṉ vīraṉ eṉpataik kuṟikkum
       piṉṉiṣ col sankari, pāṭakaṉ eṉṉum poruḷuḷḷa paḻaiya nōrṭik (Old
       Nordic) collāṉa sangare varai piṉ nōkkic celkiṟataik kavaṉittal
       maṉatil ārvattai ēṟpaṭuttum. vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉiṉ cakti vāynta coṟkaḷ
       etirāḷiyaic cēṟṟil amiḻa vaikkiṟatu (mukappu paṭattukkāṉa viḷakkam
       pārkka). vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉiṉ pāttirap paṇpai eḷimaiyāṉa muṟaikaḷil
       terintuk koḷḷap palvēṟu kalvimāṉkaḷ eṭutta muyaṟcikaḷ mikavum
       vittiyācamāṉa māṟupāṭāṉa muṭivukaḷaiyē tantaṉa. kalēvalāvil varum
       vēṟu pala pāttiraṅkaḷukkum itu poruntum. intiyāviṉ kāviyaṅkaḷiṉ
       āyvilum ittakaiya nilamai nikaḻvataik kāṇalām: utāraṇamāka,
       ēṟkaṉavē muṟkālattil irunta palvēṟu tēvarkaḷiṉ maṟupiṟappē

       tamiḻ moḻipeyarppu
       immoḻipeyarppu muṟṟup peṟṟapoḻutu nāṉ innāṭṭukku vantu patiṉōru
       āṇṭukaḷ muṭivaṭaintuviṭṭaṉa. eṉavē ivvēlaiyait toṭaṅkiya camayam
       eṉakku innāṭṭu vāḻkkaiyum moḻiyum kalācāramum ōraḷavu
       paḻakkappaṭṭuviṭṭaṉa. anta tuṇiccalil tāṉ ippāriya paṇiyait
       toṭaṅkiṉēṉ. āṉāl pōkap pōkattāṉ ataṉ cumai terintatu. intiyāvilō
       ilaṅkaiyilō aṉṟēl ulakiṉ vēṟenta mulaiyilō vāḻum oru vācakarukku
       immoḻipeyarppuk kiṭaikkumāyiṉ, avvācakar muṉpiṉ kaṇṭu kēṭṭu aṟiyāta
       inta nāṭṭup paḻakka vaḻakkaṅkaḷai, tāvaraṅkaḷai, uyiriṉaṅkaḷaip
       paṟṟi evvāṟu puriya vaikkap pōkiṟēṉ eṉṟa malaippu ēṟpaṭṭatu. iṉṟu
       inta nāṭṭil aṟupatu, eḻupatu tamiḻarkaḷ varaiyil nāṭeṅkum citaṟi
       vāḻkiṉṟa pōtilum, avarkaḷiṭaiyē tamiḻaṟivuṭaiyōraik kāṇpatu

       nūlkaḷ, akarātikaḷ ākiyavaṟṟaip peṟuvataṟkillai. inta nūl

       pāṭal 9l vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉukkuk kāyam ēṟpaṭṭu irattam perukukiṟatu.

       irattamē nilmuṉ etircuva raippōl!
       mikucōri āṟē vēliyaip pōlnil!
       āḻiyil niṟkum vāḷeṉa niṟpāy!
       koḻuñcē ṟeḻunta kōraip pulleṉa!
       vayalilē uḷḷa varampiṉaip pōlnil!
       nīrvīḻcci yiluṟu neṭuṅkal eṉanil!
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ tāṉ maṇam ceyvataṟku vaṭa nāṭṭu maṅkaiyaik
       kēṭpataṟkāka oru paṭakil puṟappaṭṭuc celkiṟāṉ. itaiyaṟinta
       ilmariṉaṉ taṉum oru caṟukku vaṇṭiyil viraikiṟāṉ. orē peṇṇukkup
       pōṭṭiyiṭṭa iru māppiḷḷaikaḷum vaḻiyil oruvaraiyoruvar
       cantikkiṉṟaṉar. iruvaratu maṉanilaiyum eppaṭi iruntirukkum? itō itu
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉiṉ kūṟṟu:
       "naṭpuṭaṉ paṭikkai nāṉoṉ ṟamaippēṉ
       peṇpala vantamāy peyartteḻal illai
       avaḷnacaik ketirmaṇam āvatum illai,
       avaṉuk kēpeṇ aḷittiṭal vēṇṭum
       avaṉuk kēavaḷ evaṉai virumpilum,

       varucel vantaṉai maṇappataṟ killai
       uyarmā ṉiṭaaṟi voruporu ḷalla
       iṉināṉ maṇappatu eḻilnuta luṭaiyōṉ
       aṅkam muḻuvatum aḻaku paṭaittōṉ;
       itunāḷ varaiyil ēntiḻai yāraiyum
       viyaṉporu ḷukkāy viṟṟatē illai
       maṅkaiyait tāṉamāy vaḻaṅkutal nallatu ... ..." ஖ pāṭal 18.

       piṉṉiṣ moḻiyil vilja 'vilyā' eṉṟa collukkut tāṉiyam eṉṟu poruḷ.
       intac col itē poruḷil iṉṟum vaḻakkil irukkiṟatu. āṉāl 'kalēvalā'
       kāviyattil iccol pala iṭaṅkaḷil 'celvam' eṉṟa potuc poruḷiltāṉ
       varukiṟatu. āṅkāṅku intac celvam eṉṉa eṉṟu pārttāl
       piṉvaruvaṉavaṟṟil ētāvatu oṉṟaittāṉ atu kuṟikkum: tāṉiyam,
       kālnaṭai, vēṭṭaiyiṉ ilakku. vāḻkkaikku mukkiyamāṉa tāṉiyam eṉṉum
       'vilyā' maṉitaṉiṉ celvamāyiṟṟu. piṉṉar vāḻkkaikku mukkiyamāṉa ellām
       [u.m. kālnaṭai] celvam vilja eṉṟu karutappaṭṭatu.
       tamiḻarum paṇṭaik kālattil kālnaṭaiyaic celvamākavē karutiṉar. māṭu
       eṉṟāl celvam eṉṟa oru poruḷum uṇṭu. antak kālattilēyē celvap
       poruḷākak kālnaṭaikaḷ amainta kāraṇattiṉālēyē atu camutāyattilē
       pōrukkuk kālāyamaintatu. pacuvaik kavaruvōrum, kavaruvōrait taṭukka
       muṉaipavarum mūrkkamāṉa pōril īṭupaṭṭaṉar eṉṟu caṅka ilakkiyaṅkaḷ
       cāṟṟukiṉṟaṉa. ikkāviyattilum vaṭanilat talaivikkum kalēvalā
       iṉattavarukkum pakai ēṟpaṭṭa kaṭṭattil kalēvalāp pakutiyiṉ
       payirkaḷaiyum kālnaṭaikaḷaiyum aḻippēṉ eṉṟu vaṭanilat talaivi kūṟi
       avvitamē ceyyavum muyalkiṟāḷ. avaḷ kūṟṟu varumāṟu:
       "kaviṉpacum puṟṟaraik karaṭiyai eḻuppuvēṉ
       periyapaṟ pirāṇiyai naṟuntēva tāruvil
       . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .
       kālnaṭai yāvaiyum kaviḻttē vīḻttiṭa
       pacuviṉam muḻutaiyum parappiṭac citaṟi;
       aṉaittumak kaḷaiyum aḻippēṉ nōyiṉāl
       uṉṟaṉ iṉattai oṉṟilā toḻippēṉ,
       eṉṟumē inta eḻilnila vuḷavarai
       avarkaḷaip paṟṟi avaṉiyil pēcceḻā(tu)." - pāṭal 43.
       'campō'vai iḻantatāl āttiram koṇṭa vaṭanilat talaivi kalēvalā
       makkaḷukkup palavitamāṉa tollaikaḷaik koṭukkiṟāḷ. mutalil
       vaḻakkattil illāta koṭiya nōykaḷai aṉuppukiṟāḷ. aṭuttu oru
       karaṭiyai ēvi viṭukiṟāḷ. muṉṟāvatākac cūriya cantirarkaḷait tiruṭi
       oru malaiyil oḷittu viṭukiṟāḷ. ataṉāl iravu vantatu; iṭaiviṭā
       tiruntatu. iruḷ niṟaintatu; iravatē nīṇṭatu. kalēvalāp pakutiyil
       kaṭumiruḷāṉatu. cūriya cantirarkaḷ eṅkē maṟaintaṉar eṉṟē teriyāta
       nilaiyil, vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ tiruvuḷac cīṭṭu mulam uṇmaiyai aṟiya
       muṟpaṭukiṟāṉ. pūrcca marattil tuṇṭukaḷ cīvi, avaṟṟai oḻuṅkāka
       aṭukkit tiruppi, iṟaivaṉaip pirārttittu uṇmaiyai aṟikiṟāṉ. itu
       vaiṉāmoyiṉaṉ kūṟṟu:
       "āṇṭava riṭamoru aṉumati kēṭkiṟēṉ
       ām,cari yāmpatil atuvoṉ ṟavaciyam:
       iṟaivaṉiṉ cīṭṭē iyampuvāy uṇmai
       tēvaṉiṉ tiruvuḷac cīṭṭē yuraippāy
       ceṅkatir emaiviṭ ṭeṅkē ceṉṟatu
       eṅkē maṟaintatu emaiviṭ ṭēmati?

       maṉitaṉiṉ viruppupōl maṟṟunī uraiyēl
       uṇmaic ceytiyai uṭaṉiṅ karuḷvāy
       vitittatu etuvō viḷampuvāy ataṉai!
       tiruvuḷac cīṭṭuc ceppiṉāl poyyurai
       cīṭṭiṉ makimaic ciṟappatāl kuṟaiyum
       tiruvuḷac cīṭṭēt tīyiṭai vīcuvar
       maṉitariṉ rācikaḷ maṟṟeri paṭalām." -pāṭal 49

       naṉṟi
       intat tamiḻ moḻipeyarppu, muṉṉiṇaippukaḷ, piṉṉiṇaippukaḷ yāvum
       cērntu cumār āyiram pakkaṅkaḷ vantaṉa. itai ōr uyartara
       patippākavum veḷiyiṭa virumpiṉōm. āyiram pakkaṅkaḷ koṇṭa oru nūl
       ciṟanta muṟaiyilum patippikkappaṭṭāl, oru carācari vācakaṉ atai
       vāṅka muṭiyāta vilaiyil viṟka nēriṭum. ataṉāl koñcam periya aḷavu
       kākitattil, oru pakkattil iraṇṭu pattikaḷil (columns) ciṟiya
       eḻuttukaḷil pāṭalkaḷai veḷiyiṭṭup pakkaṅkaḷai 500 aḷavil koṇṭuvarat
       tīrmāṉikkappaṭṭatu. attuṭaṉ innūl veḷiyīṭṭukku oru māṉiyam kōri
       piṉṉiṣ ilakkiya maṉṟattaiyum avarkaḷ mūlamākak kalvi amaiccaiyum
       helciṅkip palkalaikkaḻakam aṇukiyatu. nūṟu tamiḻarkaḷ kūṭa vāḻāta
       inta nāṭṭil āyiram pakkaṅkaḷ koṇṭa oru tamiḻ nūlai veḷiyiṭa avarkaḷ
       uṟcākattuṭaṉ utavikku vantārkaḷ.
       ivvaḷavukkum piṉṉaṇiyil niṉṟu ellāvaṟṟaiyum iyakki niṟaivu
       paṭuttiyavar pērāciriyar askō pārpolā (Dr. Asko Parpola) avarkaḷ.
       ivarē helciṅkip palkalaikkaḻakattiṉ 'āciya āpirikka nāṭukaḷ
       toṭarpāṉa kalvit tiṇaikkaḷa'ttiṉ intiyaviyal campantappaṭṭa
       kalvikkup poṟuppāṉavar. immoḻipeyarppukku maṉamuvantu ōr aṟimuka
       urai eḻutiyirukkiṟār. eṉatu iṉiya naṇpar.
       intak kāviyattiṉ vēṟu moḻipeyarppukaḷil cila urainaṭaiyilum cila
       kavitai naṭaiyilum veḷivantirukkiṉṟaṉa. tamiḻilum urainaṭaiyāka
       veḷiyiṭuvatu eṉṟa tiṭṭattuṭaṉtāṉ toṭaṅkappaṭṭatu. āṉāl piṉṉiṣ nūl
       kavitaiyaṭikaḷāka iruntatāl, antac ciṟiya aṭikaḷait tamiḻil
       ākkiyapōtu, tamiḻ moḻipeyarppum iyalpākavē kavitainaṭaiyil
       amaintuviṭṭatu. āṉāl pala iṉap pākkaḷum kalantu iruntaṉa.
       kaviñar timilaittumilaṉ inta moḻipeyarppu muḻuvataiyum paṭittup
       pārttu marapuk kavitaikaḷāka amaikkac ceyta utavi makattāṉatu.
       yāppamaitiyōṭu irunta moḻipeyarppu aṭikaḷ tavira, ēṉaiya ērāḷamāṉa
       aṭikaḷai yāppukkēṟpat tiruttic cīrkaḷuḷ carivara amaittut tantār.
       yāḻppāṇam palkalaikkaḻakattiṉ tamiḻp pērāciriyar kā. civattampi
       avarkaḷ piṉlāntukku vantirunta camayam intat tamiḻ moḻipeyarppiṉ
       cila pāṭalkaḷaip pārvaiyiṭṭut taṉatu karuttukaḷaiyum
       ālōcaṉaikaḷaiyum aṉpuṭaṉ kūṟiṉār.
       aluvakattukkup pōy maṇikkūṭṭaip pārttukkoṇṭu eṭṭu maṇi nēra vēlai
       ceytiruntāl inta moḻipeyarppu ippoḻutum muṭintirātu. itaṉ perum
       pakutiyai vīṭṭil iruntē ceytu muṭittēṉ. caṉi, ñāyiṟu, viṭumuṟai
       eṉṟa ōyvillāmal kālai nālu maṇiyiliruntu iravu pattup patiṉoru
       maṇivarai uḻaitta nāṭkaḷ ettaṉaiyō. appoḻutellām tēvaiyāṉa
       ottuḻaippait tantu eṉṉai ūkkappaṭuttiya eṉ maṉaiviyaiyum
       makkaḷaiyum iṅku kaṭṭāyam kuṟippiṭa vēṇṭum.
       mēṟkūṟiya aṉaivarukkum eṉatu naṉṟiyaic camarppaṇam ceykiṟēṉ.

       "navilvakai yāviṉum nāṉoru maṉitaṉ
       ciṟumā ṉuṭaṉtāṉ ceṟipuṉaṟ cakti    140
       cintura marattaic citaikkavē vantēṉ
       ataṉait tuṇṭiṭ ṭaḻikkavē vantēṉ."
       nilaipeṟum mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ
       intac coṟkaḷil iyampiṉaṉ avaṉē:
       "eṇṇavē illaināṉ uṉṉaip paṭaittatum
       uṉṉaip paṭaittu uṉaiyaṉup piyatum
       valiya cintura maramviḻaṟ kalla
       māperum marattai māyppataṟ kalla."
       avvita māka avaṉpakar vēḷai
       mīṇṭum pārvaiyai vīciya poḻutu    150
       maṉitaṉō muṟṟilum māṟiṭak kaṇṭāṉ
       vīraṉō putiyaṉāy viḷaṅkiṭak kaṇṭāṉ.
       pātaṅkaḷ taraiyiṟ patintu niṉṟaṉa
       talaiyō nimirntu mukilait toṭṭatu
       tāṭiyō muḻuṅkāl taṉṉilum tāḻntatu
       talaimayir kutivarai taḻaittunīṇ ṭiruntatu
       iruviḻik kiṭaiyē iṭaiveḷi āṟaṭi
       kāṟcaṭ ṭaiyatum kāṟpuṟam āṟaṭi
       muḻaṅkāliṉ pakkamum muḻumaiyāy oṉṟarai
       iṭuppiṉ cuṟṟaḷa viraṇṭā yiruntatu.    160
       kaiviral kōṭarik kāmpiṉil vaittu
       alakiṉait tīṭṭi ākkiṉaṉ kūrmai
       āṟu kaṟkaḷil ataṉait tīṭṭiṉaṉ
       ēḻām kallilum iṉṉum tīṭṭiṉaṉ.
       eḻuntāṉ nakarntāṉ etirppuṟam vantāṉ
       iṉṉum culapamāy muṉṉē naṭantāṉ
       avaṉkāṟ caṭṭai akaṉṟa uṭaiyoṭu
       virintakāṟ caṭṭai paranta uṭaiyoṭu;
       mutalaṭi eṭuttu muṉṉē vaittāṉ
       meṉmaikoṇ ṭamainta mikumaṇaṟ parappil,    170
       iraṇṭaṭi vaittu iṉṉumuṉ vantu
       **īral niṟattil iyaintamaṇ ṇaṭaintāṉ,
       muṉṟām aṭiyai muyaṉṟumēl vaittu
       cintū rattiṉ ceṟivē raṭaintāṉ.
       kōṭari koṇṭē koṭumaram tākki
       ōṅki veṭṭiṉaṉ uyarkū ralakāl
       orumuṟai veṭṭi irumuṟai veṭṭiṉaṉ
       mūṉṟām muṟaiyum muyaṉṟāṉ viraivāy;
       aṉaṟpoṟi kōṭari alakil eḻuntatu
       cintura marattil tīppoṟi teṟittatu,     180
       nontu carintu vantatu cinturam
       aṭaṟperu maramum āṭṭaṅ kaṇṭatu.
       muyaṉṟaav vāṟamai mūṉṟām muṟaiyil
       cintura marattait tīrttuk kaṭṭiṉaṉ
       peruvali maramum peyarntu vīḻntatu
       nūṟām kiḷaikaḷait tuṇipaṭac ceytatu.
       kiḻakkē aṭimarat tuṇṭaik kiṭattiṉaṉ
       vaṭamēṟ kiṉilē maramēṟ pakuti
       vaṭakkē ilaitaḻai vantuvīḻn tatuvāl
       komparkaḷ vaṭakkil kuvintuvīḻn taṉavē.    190
       avaṉoru kiḷaiyiṉai aṅkaiyi leṭuttu
       ellaiyil lāta iṉpam peṟṟāṉ;
       ucci marattai oṭittāṉ eḷitāy
       muṭivilā māya vittaiyaik kaṇṭāṉ;
       ilaiyuṟum kiḷaiyai eṭuttāṉ taṉittu
       tiṭamām aṉpait toṭṭeṭut tiṭṭāṉ.
       eñciya citaivukaḷ eṅkum citaṟiṉa
       miñcik kiṭanta mikumarat tuṇṭukaḷ
       uyarnta teḷinta oḷirkaṭal mēlē
       parantu virinta paṭaralai kaḷiṉmēl    200
       kāṟṟu vantutā lāṭṭic ceṉṟatu
       kaṭalalai eḻuntu kaṭattic ceṉṟatu
       tiṟanta nīrilē cellum tōṇipōl
       alaikaṭal mītu alaiyum kappalpōl.
       vaṭanā ṭavaṟṟai vāyu cumantatu;
       vaṭanilac **ciṟupeṇ maṅkaiyāṅ korutti
       ciṟuciṟu tuṇikaḷaic cemmaiyāyt tōyttāḷ
       uṭaikaḷaik kaḻuvi ularavum vaittāḷ
       nīriṉ karaiyil nilaittapā ṟaiyilē
       nīṇṭa karaiyil nilavum muṉaiyil.    210
       mitantatuṇ ṭukaḷai melliyaḷ kaṇṭāḷ
       kaippai oṉṟilē kavaṉamāyc cērttāḷ
       paiyiṉai yeṭuttup paṭarntil laṭaintāḷ
       muṉṟilil vaittāḷ muḻunīḷ piṭippai
       māyavit taikku valucaram ceyaṟku
       paṭaikkalam mantirap paṇikkāyc ceyya.
       cintura marattaic citaittapō tiṉilē
       tīyaat taruvum tīrttaḻin tatuvāl
       katiravaṉ mīṇṭum katirkaḷait tantāṉ
       tiṅkaḷiṉ nīḷnilā tirumpivan tiṭṭatu     220
       mēkam nīṇṭu mēlē mitantatu
       vāṉavil viṇṇil vaḷaintu niṉṟatu
       pukārpaṭi kaṭalataṉ puṇarmuṉai nuṉiyil
       ceṟipaṉip pukāruḷa tīvataṉ karaiyil.
       muḻutāyk kāṭukaḷ muḷaittut taḻaittaṉa
       vaṉaṅkaḷ ceḻittu vaḷarntiṭa lāyiṉa
       tarukkaḷil ilaikaḷ, taraiyiṉiṟ puṟkaḷ,
       maraṅkaḷiṟ pāṭi makiḻpuṭ kulaṅkaḷ,
       pāṭum paṟavaikaḷ paravacap paṭṭaṉa
       marattiṉ ucciyil **maṇikkuyil kūviṉa.    230
       pūmiyiṟ ciṟupaḻac ceṭikaḷ polintaṉa
       vayalkaḷil poṉṉiṟap pūkkaḷ malarntaṉa
       palliṉap puṟkaḷum pāṅkāy muḷaittaṉa
       vaḷarntaṉa paṟpala vaṭivaṅ kaḷilē;
       āyiṉum pārli aṅkeḻa villai
       aritām appayir atumuḷait tilatē.
       mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ ataṉpiṉ
       eṅkum naṭantē eṇṇip pārttāṉ
       nīlak kaṭaliṉ nīḷkarai taṉṉil
       māperuṅ kaṭaliṉ maṭiviṉ ellaiyil;    240
       āṟutāṉiya arumaṇi kaṇṭāṉ
       ēḻu vitaikaḷai eṭuttaṉaṉ āṅkē
       āḻiyiṉ ellai yāmparap piṭattē
       maṇal niṟaintiṭṭa vaṇkarai mītu
       avaṟṟaik **kīriyiṉ aruntōl vaittāṉ
       kōṭai aṇiliṉ kuṟuṅkā laṭiyil.
       vitaikaḷai nilattil vitaikkac ceṉṟāṉ
       ceṉṟāṉ tāṉiyac ceḻumvitai tūva
       *kalēvalāp pakutiyiṉ kaṟkiṇaṟ ṟarukē
       *osamō viṉvayal uḷaviḷai viṭattē.    250
       maramicai yorupuḷ vāytiṟan ticaittatu:
       "osamōviṉ pārli oṉṟumē muḷaiyā
       kalēvalāviṉ **payiṟum kaviṉuṟa vaḷarā
       maṇṇaik kiḷaṟip paṇcey yāviṭil
       aṭarvaṉa maraṅkaḷai aḻitti ṭāviṭil
       aḻitta maraṅkaḷai eritti ṭāviṭil."
       nilaipeṟum mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ
       kūriya alakuk kōṭari ceytāṉ
       viriperum pakutiyai veṭṭiyē aḻittāṉ
       maṇṇaik kiḷaṟip paṇcey tiṭṭāṉ    260
       viyaṉmara maṉaittaiyum veṭṭi vīḻttiṉaṉ
       taṉimilā ṟoṉṟē tavirntu niṉṟatu
       paṟavai yiṉaṅkaḷ pāṅkuṟat taṅka
       kuyilvan tiruntu kūvutaṟ kāka.
       viṇṇilōr kaḻuku vīccā yeḻuntatu
       viṇṇakam muḻuvatum mēvip paṟantatu
       ataṉaip pārkka avviṭam vantatu:
       "immaram maṭṭumēṉ iṅkuniṟ kiṟatu?
       milāṟu marattaiyēṉ vīḻttiṭa villai?
       eḻiluṟu maramitai ēṉveṭ ṭiṭavi(l)lai?"   270
       mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ moḻintāṉ:
       "immaram maṭṭumiṅ kiruppataṉ kāraṇam:
       paṟavaikaḷ vantu pāṅkāyt taṅka
       vāṉak kaḻuku vantunaṉ kamara."
       viṇṇakap paṟavai viṟaṟkaḻu kuraittatu:
       "iṅkunī nallatōr iruntoḻil ceytaṉai:
       milāṟu marattiṉai veṭṭā tiṭṭaṉai
       uṉṉatat taruvai uyarntiṭa viṭṭaṉai
       paṟavaikaḷ vantu pāṅkuṟat taṅka
       nāṉē vantu naṉṟā yamara."     280
       viṇṇakap paṟavaitī viraintumuṭ ṭiṟṟu
       eriyiṉai yōṅki eḻuntiṭa vaittatu
       vaṭakkiṉ kāṟṟu vaṉkā ṭaḻittatu
       vaṭakīḻk kāṟṟum maraṅkaḷai yuṇṭatu
       ellā maraṅkaḷum erintu muṭintaṉa
       cāmpalāy māṟit takartuka ḷāyiṉa.
       appō mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ
       āṟu vitaikaḷai aḷḷiṉāṉ oṉṟāy
       ēḻu vitaikaḷai eṭuttāṉ kaiyil
       kīrit tōlataṉ kīḻē yiruntu     290
       kōṭai aṇiliṉ kāliṭai yiruntu
       kōṭaip **pirāṇiyiṉ koḻuṅkā liruntu.
       piṟpā ṭavaṉnilam vittiṭap pōṉāṉ
       tāṉiya maṇikaḷait tūvic citaṟi
       intac coṟkaḷil iyampiṉaṉ avaṉē:
       "vitaikkap pōkiṟēṉ nāṉiv vitaikaḷai
       paṭaippiṉ periyōṉ paṇaivira liruntu
       cakalatum vallōṉ taṉkarat tiruntu
       intap puvimicai ivaimuḷait tuyara
       vaḷarntu ceḻittu vaḷamuṭaṉ palka.    300
       puviyiṉ kīḻeḻil polimutu makaḷē!
       maṇṇiṉ maṅkai, mānilat talaivi!
       muḷaiyai veḷiyē muḷaittuya raccey
       ceḻittu varaccey tikaḻmaṇ tuṇaiyāl;
       nilattiṉ cakti nitampoyk kātu
       intap pūmi irukkum varaikkum,
       tantavaṉ aṉpu tarittirup pataṉāl
       iyaṟkaiyiṉ makaḷiṉ iruntuṇai yataṉāl.
       maṇṇē, eḻuvāy vaḷartuyi liruntu!
       paṭaippōṉ puṟkaḷē, paṭukkaiyi liruntu!   310
       taṇṭukaḷ eḻaṭṭum taraiyiṉaik kiḻittu!
       kāmpukaḷ kiḷarntu kaṭukaniṉ ṟiṭaṭaṭṭum!
       katirmaṇi yāyiram kaviṉoṭu varaṭṭum!
       nūṟunū ṟākak kiḷaipaṭarn tiṭaṭṭum!
       eṉatu uḻaviṉil, eṉatu vitaippiṉil,
       eṉatu uḻaippiṟ kiyaiyū tiyamāy!
       ō, maṉu mutalva, uyarmāteyvamē!
       viṇṇakam vāḻum mēlvakait tantaiyē!
       mukiṟkulam purakkum mutukā valaṉē!
       nīrāvi aṉaittaiyum nitamāḷ pavaṉē!    320
       mukilkaḷ yāvaiyum kuvivuṟa vaṇaittu
       cantikka vaippāy tavaḻvāṉ veḷiyil,
       iyakkuvāy kiḻakkil iruntoru mukilai
       vaṭamēṟ koṉṟu vantutik kaṭṭum
       mēṟki liruntu mikutiyai aṉuppu
       aṉupputeṟ kiruntum ativirai vāka
       tolvā ṉiruntu tūṟalkaḷ varaṭṭum
       eḻiliyi liruntutēṉ tuḷikaḷ viḻaṭṭum
       mutupuvi muḷaikkum muḷaikaḷiṉ mītu
       uyirpeṟ ṟuyarum payirkaḷiṉ mītu."    330
       māṉiṭa mutalvaṉ, māperun tēvaṉ
       nilaipeṟum cuvark kamāḷ nēcat tantai
       mēkam mēluṟum viyaṉcapai yamarpavar
       uyarntu teḷinta uyarmaṉ ṟuṟaipavar;
       orumukil kiḻakkirun tuṭaṉ varuvittār
       vaṭamēṟ kiruntoru maḻaimukil paṭaittār
       iṉṉoṉṟai mēṟkē iruntum aṉuppiṉār
       aṉaittaiyum teṟkirun tavarvirai vittār
       aṉaittaiyum oṉṟucērt taḻuttit taḷḷiṉār
       oṉṟuṭaṉ oṉṟai urāyap piṇittār.    340
       vāṉat tiruntu māriyaip poḻintār
       tēṉtuḷi teḷittār tiraḷmukil ataṉāl
       muḷaittu vanta muḷaikaḷiṉ mēlē
       uyirpeṟ ṟuyarnta payirkaḷiṉ mēlē.
       kūrmuṉai oṉṟu kutittumē leḻuntatu
       iḷantūr oṉṟu itamāy vaḷarntatu
       pacumai vayaliṉ paṭarnilat tiruntu
       vaiṉā moyiṉaṉiṉ kaiviṉait tiṟattāl.
       iraṇṭu nāṭkaḷ iṉitu muṭintaṉa
       iraṇṭām muṉṟām iravukaḷ pōyiṉa    350
       muḻumaiyāy vāram oṉṟu muṭintapiṉ
       nilaipeṟum mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ
       vaḷarcciyaip pārkka valamāy vantāṉ
       tāṉē uḻutu tāṉē vitaitta
       ūkkat tuṟupalaṉ nōkkutaṟ kāka;
       virumpiya vāṟē viḷaintatu pārli
       āṟu vaḻikaḷil akalkatir ceṟintu
       taṇṭukaḷ mutticai tāmē pirintu.
       mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉu māṅkē
       celuttiṉāṉ pārvai tirumpiṉāṉ cuṟṟi    360
       kulavum vacantak kōkilam vantatu
       vaḷarnta milāṟiṉai vantatum kaṇṭatu:
       "immaram māttiram ēṉiṅku niṉṟatu?
       milāṟu maṭṭumēṉ vīḻātu niṉṟatu?"
       mutiya vaiṉā moyiṉaṉ moḻintāṉ:
       "immaram maṭṭumēṉ iṅkuniṟ kiṟateṉil
       milāṟu maṭṭumēṉ vīḻntiṭa vilaiyeṉil
       uṉakkāy nīvan tuṟaivataṟ kāka;
       kuyilē kuyilē kūvuvāy ippō
       iṉiya neñcāl iṉpamāyp pāṭu.    370
       veṇpoṉ neñcāl mikupāṭ ṭicaippāy
       **īyanaṉ neñcāl eḻiṟpā vicaippāy
       kālaiyil pāṭu, mālaiyil pāṭu,
       naṇpakal orumuṟai nalamikap pāṭu
       eṉviḷai nilamirum makiḻcciyil tiḷaikka
       eṉatukā ṉakamellām eḻiloṭē vaḷara
       taṭamellām tāḻvilāc ceḻippiṉil koḻikka
       eṉatumā nilamellām eḻilvaḷam poliya."
